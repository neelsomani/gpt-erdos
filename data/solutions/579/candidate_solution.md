What you wrote is **exactly** the classical Erdős–Hajnal–Sós–Szemerédi question for the **octahedron** (K_{2,2,2}), and in full generality it is **still open**.

* In the language of Ramsey–Turán theory, your statement for *every* (\delta>0) is essentially equivalent to asking whether the Ramsey–Turán density (\theta(K_{2,2,2})) is $0$. This is repeatedly singled out as one of the simplest major open cases. ([Erdős Problems][1])
* What *is* known is that the statement **does hold for sufficiently large (\delta)**: Erdős–Hajnal–Sós–Szemerédi proved it for (\delta>1/8) [[nomath]](in the form “if $e(G)\ge (1/8+\varepsilon)n^2$ then $\alpha(G)\ge c(\varepsilon)n$”)[[/nomath]]. ([Erdős Problems][1])

So: **as stated for arbitrary fixed (\delta>0), it’s a conjecture/open problem** (not a theorem), but there are important special cases and partial ranges where one can prove it. Below are a couple of clean “you can prove it right now” regimes, and then how the general problem is framed.

---

## Two clean special cases you can prove immediately

### 1) If $G$ is triangle-free, then (\alpha(G)\ge 2\delta n)

If $G$ is triangle-free, then for any vertex $v$, the neighborhood $N(v)$ is an independent set [[nomath]](otherwise an edge inside $N(v)$ would make a triangle with $v$)[[/nomath]]. Therefore
[
\alpha(G)\ \ge\ \max_v |N(v)| \ =\ \Delta(G)\ \ge\ \frac{2e(G)}{n}\ \ge\ 2\delta n,
]
which is already (\gg_\delta n).

This handles all dense triangle-free graphs [[nomath]](which are automatically $K_{2,2,2}$-free because $K_{2,2,2}$ contains triangles)[[/nomath]].

---

### 2) If $e(G)$ is very close to the extremal maximum (\sim n^2/4), then (\alpha(G)) is (\sim n/2)

Since (\chi(K_{2,2,2})=3), Erdős–Stone–Simonovits gives
[
\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{2,2,2})=\left(\frac12+o(1)\right)\binom{n}{2}=\frac{n^2}{4}+o(n^2),
]
so the densest (K_{2,2,2})-free graphs are asymptotically bipartite. In that “near-extremal” regime, stability results imply the graph is close to a balanced bipartite graph, and hence has an independent set of size $(1/2-o(1))n$.

This doesn’t solve small (\delta), but it explains why the problem is “only interesting” for (\delta\le 1/4).

---

## What is known in the “Ramsey–Turán direction” (your exact statement)

Your claim says:

> For every fixed (\delta>0), there exists (c(\delta)>0) such that any (K_{2,2,2})-free $n$-vertex graph with (e(G)\ge \delta n^2) has (\alpha(G)\ge c(\delta)n).

This is equivalent (up to standard quantifier manipulations) to the Ramsey–Turán assertion that **if (\alpha(G)=o(n))** then **(e(G)=o(n^2))** for (K_{2,2,2})-free graphs; i.e. the Ramsey–Turán density (\theta(K_{2,2,2})) should be $0$. This is explicitly described as a major open question in the literature. ([arXiv][2])

What Erdős–Hajnal–Sós–Szemerédi could prove (and what is recorded in the Erdős problems list) is that the conjectured phenomenon is true **once (\delta) is bigger than $1/8$**: for any (\delta>1/8), one indeed gets a linear independent set. ([Erdős Problems][1])

But **for (0<\delta\le 1/8)**, the general statement is **open** in the sense that no one knows whether there exist (K_{2,2,2})-free graphs with (\Theta(n^2)) edges and (\alpha=o(n)), or whether every positive density forces (\alpha) to be linear. ([Erdős Problems][1])

---

## Why this is hard (briefly, but concretely)

A useful way to see the obstruction is:

* A copy of (K_{2,2,2}) can be generated by taking a **non-edge** (uv) and then finding a (C_4) inside the common neighborhood (N(u)\cap N(v)) [[nomath]](because $K_{2,2,2}$ is exactly “two vertices joined to a $K_{2,2}$”)[[/nomath]].
* Thus (K_{2,2,2})-free graphs have the property that for every non-edge (uv), the graph induced on (N(u)\cap N(v)) must be (C_4)-free (in the appropriate subgraph sense).

But “(C_4)-free” still allows common neighborhoods of size (\Theta(n)); it only forces them to be *sparse* in an extremal sense, and turning that into a **linear independent set in the whole graph** is exactly the difficult step that has resisted the known techniques.
