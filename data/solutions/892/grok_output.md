# Grok Response for Problem 892

**On the existence of primitive sequences majorized by a given sequence**

A sequence \(A = (a_n)_{n=1}^\infty\) of positive integers is called *primitive* if \(a_i \nmid a_j\) whenever \(i \neq j\). The notation \(a_n \ll b_n\) is interpreted in the classical Erdős sense as \(a_n = o(b_n)\) (i.e., \(a_n/b_n \to 0\)). Thus the question asks for a necessary and sufficient condition on a strictly increasing sequence \(B = (b_n)\) guaranteeing the existence of a primitive sequence \(A\) satisfying \(a_n = o(b_n)\) for all \(n\).

We first derive a necessary condition by recalling the fundamental density restriction on primitive sequences. Erdős (1935) proved that there exists an absolute constant \(C > 0\) (in fact \(C = e^\gamma\) works, where \(\gamma\) is the Euler–Mascheroni constant, by later refinements) such that
\[
\sum_{a \in A} \frac{1}{a \log a} \le C
\]
for any primitive set \(A \subset \mathbb{N}\) with all elements at least 3. (The sum is taken over the elements themselves, not indexed by \(n\).) Suppose a primitive \(A\) with \(a_n = o(b_n)\) exists. Then, for any fixed \(\varepsilon > 0\), there is \(N\) such that \(a_n < \varepsilon b_n\) for all \(n \ge N\). Splitting the sum and using monotonicity,
\[
\sum_{n \ge N} \frac{1}{a_n \log a_n} > \frac{1}{\log(\varepsilon b_N)} \sum_{n \ge N} \frac{1}{a_n}.
\]
If \(B\) grows slowly enough that \(\sum 1/b_n = \infty\) at a rate forcing the left-hand side above to exceed \(C\) (after adjusting constants), no such primitive \(A\) can exist. More precisely, a necessary condition is that
\[
\sum_n \frac{1}{b_n \log b_n} < \infty
\]
or at least that the partial sums remain bounded by a multiple of \(C\) after a logarithmic shift. If the sum diverges too rapidly, the putative \(A\) would violate Erdős's bound.

This necessary condition is *not* sufficient. To see this, construct a counter-example sequence \(B\) satisfying the divergence condition yet for which every candidate primitive set eventually violates the \(o(b_n)\) requirement. Let \(B\) consist of two interleaved parts: (i) all powers of 2 (which grow exponentially), and (ii) a very dense block of consecutive integers in each dyadic interval \([2^k, 2^{k+1}]\) chosen so that their reciprocal sum is large but any primitive subset must omit at least half of them (by the standard greedy argument: if \(m\) lies in the block then no multiple \(2m\) can be chosen later). The gcd condition \((b_i, b_j) = b_k\) can be violated inside the dense blocks without affecting the overall divergence of \(\sum 1/(b_n \log b_n)\). Any primitive \(A\) that tries to keep \(a_n = o(b_n)\) is forced to pick too many elements from the dense blocks, eventually producing a divisibility relation (e.g., an element and twice that element both selected from later blocks). Thus the sum bound is satisfied but primitivity fails under the growth constraint.

The additional supposition that \(B\) admits “no non-trivial solutions to \((b_i, b_j) = b_k\)” (meaning the only solutions occur when \(\{i,j,k\}\) are not all distinct and correspond to a trivial divisibility \(b_i \mid b_j\)) does *not* guarantee existence. This condition implies that \(B\) is itself primitive and that the greatest common divisors of distinct pairs never coincide with a third term. While this prevents obvious obstructions (no term is “generated” as a gcd of two others, so the set is “gcd-free” in a strong sense), it is insufficient to force a primitive \(A\) with \(a_n = o(b_n)\). One can still embed dense blocks as above while maintaining the gcd-free property: choose the blocks inside intervals where all integers share no small prime factors in common, ensuring that pairwise gcds fall below the smallest element of \(B\) and thus lie outside \(B\). The Erdős sum bound is again satisfied, yet the same primitivity-versus-density conflict appears.

A plausible candidate for a necessary *and* sufficient condition is that the series \(\sum 1/(b_n \log b_n)\) converges to a value strictly less than the Erdős constant \(C\), *and* that the sequence \(B\) satisfies a uniform lower bound on the minimal prime factors: there exists \(\delta > 0\) such that the smallest prime factor of \(b_n\) exceeds \(b_n^\delta\) for infinitely many \(n\). This extra arithmetic condition prevents the dense-block obstructions above, because numbers with a very large prime factor can be selected greedily without creating divisibility chains. Under these hypotheses one can construct \(A\) inductively by, at stage \(k\), selecting the smallest integer \(m > a_{k-1}\) with a prime factor \(> m^{1/2}\) that lies below \(\varepsilon b_k\) (for a suitable \(\varepsilon\)) and does not divide or is not divided by previously chosen terms. The gcd-free hypothesis on \(B\) ensures that the “forbidden” multiples generated by previous choices do not intersect the admissible range for later \(b_n\) too severely, allowing the induction to continue while keeping \(a_k = o(b_k)\).

Unfortunately, proving sufficiency rigorously requires controlling the interaction between the prime-factor condition and the global sum bound across all scales, which reduces to an intricate sieving problem that remains open. The necessity of the sum bound is clear from Erdős’s theorem, but closing the gap between necessity and sufficiency appears difficult; the gcd-free hypothesis eliminates only the most elementary obstructions and does not address overlapping divisibility constraints at different scales.

After examining several constructions, sum estimates, and possible strengthenings of the gcd-free hypothesis, the author has been unable to establish a clean necessary and sufficient criterion that fully resolves the problem.
