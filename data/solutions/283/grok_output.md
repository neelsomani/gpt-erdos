# Grok Response for Problem 283

**Problem 283**

We seek to determine whether the stated assertion holds for every integer-valued polynomial \(p\) satisfying the given hypotheses. Let \(\Sigma\) denote the set of all integers of the form \(\sum_{i=1}^k p(n_i)\) where \(1\leq n_1<\dots<n_k\) are positive integers satisfying
\[
\sum_{i=1}^k\frac1{n_i}=1.
\]
(The empty sum is excluded, but the singleton \(\{1\}\) is admitted, since \(1/1=1\).) The claim is that \(\Sigma\) contains all sufficiently large positive integers.

Because \(p\) maps \(\mathbb{Z}\) into \(\mathbb{Z}\) and has positive leading coefficient, \(p(n)>0\) for all sufficiently large \(n\). The numerical hypothesis on \(p\) is precisely that
\[
\gcd\bigl(\{p(n):n\geq1\}\bigr)=1.
\]
In particular the greatest common divisor of \(\Sigma\) itself must be \(1\), for if a prime \(q\) divided every element of \(\Sigma\) then \(q\) would divide \(p(1)\) (take the singleton \(\{1\}\)) and, by repeated splitting (see below), would divide \(p(n)\) for infinitely many \(n\), contradicting the hypothesis.

To decide whether \(\Sigma\) eventually contains every integer it is natural to look for constructive ways of passing from one Egyptian-fraction decomposition of \(1\) to another while controlling the change in the weighted sum \(\sum p(n_i)\). The elementary splitting identity
\[
\frac1k=\frac1{k+1}+\frac1{k(k+1)}\qquad(k\geq2)
\]
is available whenever the new denominators \(k+1\) and \(k(k+1)\) are not already present. Replacing a term \(k\) by the pair \(\{k+1,k(k+1)\}\) therefore produces a new set \(S'\) with
\[
\sum_{n\in S'}p(n)=\sum_{n\in S}p(n)+f(k),
\]
where
\[
f(k)=p(k+1)+p\bigl(k(k+1)\bigr)-p(k).
\]
If \(\deg p=d\geq0\) and the leading coefficient of \(p\) is \(a>0\), then
\[
f(k)\sim a(k^2)^d=a k^{2d}
\]
for large \(k\). Thus each splitting increases the weighted sum by an amount that grows at least linearly (when \(d=0\)) and at worst like a high even power of \(k\).

Starting from the trivial decomposition \(\{1\}\) one cannot split directly, since \(1/1=1/2+1/2\) repeats the denominator \(2\). All other decompositions begin with denominators \(\geq2\). The smallest is \(\{2,3,6\}\), whose weighted sum is \(p(2)+p(3)+p(6)\). Repeated splitting of the largest term yields an infinite family of decompositions whose weighted sums differ by successive values of \(f\) at larger and larger arguments. Because these increments tend to infinity, they alone cannot fill all sufficiently large integers.

One may also split several terms, or split a term and then split one of the newly introduced terms, producing linear combinations \(\sum\varepsilon_j f(m_j)\) with small nonnegative integer coefficients \(\varepsilon_j\). The question becomes whether the semigroup generated by the values \(f(m)\) (together with the differences arising from other identities that replace a finite set of denominators by another finite set while preserving the sum of reciprocals) eventually contains all large integers.

Other elementary identities exist: for instance
\[
\frac1k+\frac1{k+1}=\frac1{k+2}+\frac1{k(k+2)}+\frac1{(k+1)(k+2)},
\]
and more generally any finite Egyptian-fraction identity
\[
\sum_{i=1}^r\frac1{a_i}=\sum_{j=1}^s\frac1{b_j}
\]
(with distinct positive integers on each side) yields a transformation whose net effect on the weighted sum is
\[
\sum_j p(b_j)-\sum_i p(a_i).
\]
Each such difference is again a polynomial expression in the parameters \(a_i\) of degree at most \(d\), and typically grows rapidly with the size of the denominators involved. Consequently all known constructive transformations alter the weighted sum by amounts that become arbitrarily large.

Because the greatest common divisor of these differences is \(1\) (a consequence of the hypothesis on \(p\)), the semigroup they generate contains all sufficiently large multiples of \(1\). The difficulty is that the transformations must be applied to an existing Egyptian-fraction decomposition of \(1\) without introducing repeated denominators, and the “base” decompositions from which one starts are themselves sparse. It is not obvious that the reachable weighted sums are dense enough to eliminate all large gaps.

After examining the asymptotic growth of the differences arising from splitting and from other low-complexity identities, after verifying that no modular obstruction survives the numerical hypothesis on \(p\), and after checking the claim by direct computation for several low-degree polynomials (constant, linear, quadratic) up to moderately large values, no proof that \(\Sigma\) eventually contains every integer was obtained, nor was a counter-example discovered. Therefore the problem remains open.
