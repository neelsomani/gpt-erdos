# Grok Response for Problem 387

**Problem 387**

We seek to determine whether there exists a fixed constant \(c > 0\) (independent of \(n\)) such that for every integer \(n \geq 2\) and every integer \(k\) with \(1 \leq k < n\), the binomial coefficient \(\binom{n}{k}\) admits at least one positive divisor \(d\) satisfying \(cn < d \leq n\).

To approach this, factor \(\binom{n}{k}\) as \(m \cdot t\), where \(m\) is the largest divisor of \(\binom{n}{k}\) all of whose prime factors are at most \(cn\) (i.e., the \((cn)\)-smooth part), and \(t\) is coprime to \(m\) (so all prime factors of \(t\) exceed \(cn\)). Any divisor of \(\binom{n}{k}\) that is at most \(n\) must divide \(m\) if it is to avoid prime factors larger than \(cn\), but more precisely: divisors of \(\binom{n}{k}\) that lie in \((cn, n]\) can only arise from divisors of \(m\) (since any factor incorporating a prime from \(t\) exceeds \(cn\), and if that prime exceeds \(n\) the product certainly exceeds \(n\)).

Thus, the question reduces to whether \(m\) necessarily has a divisor in \((cn, n]\). If \(m \leq cn\), then all divisors of \(m\) are at most \(cn\), so none lie in the target interval. If \(m > n\), the divisors of \(m\) might fill \((cn, n]\) or might skip it. Since \(m\) is \((cn)\)-smooth, its prime factors are all \(\leq cn\). The divisors of such an \(m\) are multiplicatively generated by these small primes (with the exact exponents given by the \(p\)-adic valuations \(v_p(\binom{n}{k})\), which are computable via de Polignac's formula:
\[
v_p(\binom{n}{k}) = \frac{s_p(k) + s_p(n-k) - s_p(n)}{p-1},
\]
where \(s_p(\cdot)\) is the sum of base-\(p\) digits). For \(m\) to skip \((cn, n]\), there must be consecutive divisors \(d_1 \leq cn < n < d_2\) (with \(d_2\) the next after \(d_1\)) such that \(d_2 / d_1 > 1/c\).

When the prime factors of \(m\) are few (e.g., \(m = q^a\) for a single prime \(q \leq cn\)), the divisors are simply \(\{1, q, q^2, \dots, q^a\}\). A gap occurs if \(q^j \leq cn < q^{j+1}\) and simultaneously \(q^{j+1} > n\), which forces \(q > 1/c\). For fixed \(c > 0\) and large \(n\), primes \(q\) with \(1/c < q \leq cn\) exist (by the prime-number theorem, the interval \((1/c, cn]\) contains \(\sim cn / \log(cn)\) primes). If the valuations \(v_q(\binom{n}{k})\) can be arranged so that the jump \(q^{j+1}\) lands beyond \(n\) while \(q^j \leq cn\), and if the cofactor \(t\) has all its primes \(> n\) (so they contribute no divisors \(\leq n\)), then \(\binom{n}{k}\) would have no divisor in \((cn, n]\).

For small fixed \(k\) (e.g., \(k=2,3\)), \(\binom{n}{k} \sim n^k / k!\) has size polynomial in \(n\). Choosing \(n\) so that \(n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)\) is divisible by high powers of a single medium-sized prime \(q \approx n^\alpha\) (with \(\alpha\) small) while the cofactor after dividing by \(k!\) is a prime larger than \(n\), produces candidates where \(m = q^a\) with the desired gap. Explicit checks for small \(n\) (e.g., \(n=6\), \(k=2\): \(\binom{6}{2}=15=3\cdot5\), divisors \(\leq 6\) are \(1,3,5\), all \(\leq 5\)) show violations for large \(c\) (here \(c > 5/6\)). Extending this to arbitrarily small \(c > 0\) requires exhibiting, for each \(c\), an \(n\) and \(k\) where the smooth part \(m\) (with primes \(\leq cn\)) has a divisor gap exceeding ratio \(1/c\) around scale \(n\), with the remaining factors all \(> n\).

When \(k \approx n/2\), Bertrand's postulate supplies primes in \((n/2, n]\), each dividing \(\binom{n}{k}\) (by Kummer's theorem: no carry when adding \(k\) and \(n-k\) in base \(p > n/2\)), yielding divisors in \((n/2, n]\). Thus central binomials satisfy the condition for any \(c < 1/2\). The difficulty lies in non-central cases, especially fixed or slowly growing \(k\) relative to \(n\), or \(k\) near boundaries where \(\binom{n}{k}\) is polynomial-like and can be engineered (via Dirichlet's theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions or sieve methods) to have factorizations with isolated medium-smooth parts exhibiting large divisor gaps.

Balancing the constraints—ensuring the \(p\)-adic valuations produce a suitable power \(q^a\) with gap > \(1/c\), while the cofactor is either 1 or a prime (or product of primes) > \(n\)—resists both constructive counterexamples for every \(c > 0\) and a uniform proof that some positive \(c\) works for all \(n, k\). Exhaustive case analysis for small \(k\) via explicit formulas (e.g., \(\binom{n}{2} = n(n-1)/2\)) shows that when \(n\) and \(n-1\) are both powers of primes slightly larger than \(cn\), gaps appear, but scaling to arbitrary \(n\) while keeping \(c\) fixed demands control over prime factors of \(n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)\) that current factorization techniques do not uniformly guarantee or refute.

After extensive examination of smooth parts, divisor distributions (via logarithmic spacing of divisors of smooth numbers), valuation formulas, and special cases (prime \(n\), primorial \(n\), consecutive smooth pairs), no resolution is obtained: neither a universal \(c > 0\) is constructed nor is it shown that no such \(c\) exists.
