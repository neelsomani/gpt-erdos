The question asks whether there exists an absolute (i.e., universal) constant \(K\) with the stated uniform property. The reduction to bounding the number of divisors of \(n\) lying in a short interval of length \(O(n^{1/4})\) near \(\sqrt{n}\) is straightforward: if \(d > \sqrt{n}\) lies in the given interval and \(d \mid n\), then the complementary divisor \(n/d < \sqrt{n}\) lies in a complementary interval of the same length immediately below \(\sqrt{n}\).

Write \(L = C n^{1/4}\) and let \(x = \sqrt{n}\). Partition the divisors in \((x, x + L)\) into two types:
- *Non-smooth*: those divisible by at least one prime \(p > L\).
- *Smooth*: those that are \(L\)-smooth.

Any prime \(p > L\) can divide at most one integer in an interval of length \(< L\), so the non-smooth divisors each contribute a distinct prime \(p_i > L\). Write each such divisor as \(d_i = s_i p_i\) (higher powers \(p_i^e\) with \(e \geq 2\) are impossible for large \(n\), since \(p_i^2 > L^2 \gg x\)). Then each \(s_i = O(L)\) and is necessarily \(L\)-smooth. The LCM of these \(d_i\) is exactly \(\operatorname{lcm}(s_1, \dots, s_r) \cdot p_1 \cdots p_r\), which divides \(n\) and is thus at most \(n\). Since each \(d_i > x\), we have
\[
\prod p_i > \frac{x^r}{\prod s_i} \geq \frac{n^{r/2}}{O(L^r)} = \Omega\left(\frac{n^{r/4}}{C^r}\right).
\]
Hence the LCM is at least \(\Omega(n^{r/4}/C^r)\). For \(r \geq 5\) and \(n\) larger than an explicit function of \(C\), this exceeds \(n\), a contradiction. Thus there are \(O(1)\) non-smooth divisors in the interval (at most 4, for \(n\) large depending on \(C\)).

If \(n\) has any prime factor \(> L\), then at most one smooth divisor \(> x\) is possible. To see this, suppose \(d_1, d_2 > x\) are \(L\)-smooth and divide \(n = n_{\mathrm{s}} \cdot n_{\mathrm{r}}\) (with \(n_{\mathrm{r}}\) the product of prime powers for primes \(> L\)). Then both divide the smooth part \(n_{\mathrm{s}} \leq n/L\), but
\[
\operatorname{lcm}(d_1, d_2) > \frac{n}{L}
\]
while also dividing \(n_{\mathrm{s}} < n/L\), which is impossible for large \(n\). Thus the total is still \(O(1)\) in this case.

The remaining case is when \(n\) itself is \(L\)-smooth (so all divisors are smooth). Here the LCM argument no longer directly yields a contradiction independent of \(C\), because the divisors share only small prime factors (\(\leq L\)) and high multiplicities are possible. While examples such as \(n = 720\) yield up to 4 divisors in such an interval for moderate \(C\), and while Dickman-function estimates show a positive proportion of integers near \(x\) are \(L\)-smooth when \(L = \Theta(x^{1/2})\), constructing (or ruling out) \(n\) with arbitrarily many divisors of \(n\) clustered in an interval of length \(C x^{1/2}\) (with \(C > 0\) arbitrarily small and \(n\) arbitrarily large) requires controlling how many points of the multiplicative subgroup generated by the primes \(\leq L\) can fall into a short additive interval after taking logarithms. Lower bounds on linear forms in logarithms give that ratios of distinct divisors cannot be *too* close to 1, but the bounds are not strong enough (given the size of the exponents, \(\approx \log n / \log 2\)) to limit the count in an interval of relative length \(n^{-1/4}\) to an absolute constant.

Thus the smooth case resists an unconditional bound independent of \(C\), and I could not resolve whether an absolute \(K\) exists.
