# Grok Response for Problem 137

**Problem 137.** Let \(k \geq 3\). Can the product of any \(k\) consecutive integers \(N\) ever be powerful? That is, must there always exist a prime \(p \mid N\) such that \(p^2 \nmid N\)?

A positive integer is *powerful* (or squareful) if every prime appearing in its factorization has exponent at least 2. Equivalently, \(N\) is powerful if and only if \(N = a^2 b^3\) for some positive integers \(a, b\).

The question asks whether \(N = n(n+1)\cdots(n+k-1)\) (with \(n \geq 1\)) can ever be powerful when \(k \geq 3\), or whether there must always be at least one prime \(p\) with \(v_p(N) = 1\).

First consider the special case \(k = 3\), so \(N = m(m^2 - 1)\) with \(m = n+1 \geq 2\). Here \(m-1\), \(m\), and \(m+1\) are pairwise coprime. If a prime \(p\) divides one of them, it cannot divide either of the other two. Consequently, if \(N\) is powerful then each of \(m-1\), \(m\), and \(m+1\) must individually be powerful (up to units; we restrict to positive integers). Thus the problem reduces to the existence of three consecutive powerful positive integers.

Among any three consecutive positive integers exactly one of the following holds:
- The pattern is even–odd–even. The two even numbers differ by 2, so one is \(\equiv 2 \pmod{4}\) (valuation \(v_2 = 1\)) while the other is \(\equiv 0 \pmod{4}\). The number with \(v_2 = 1\) cannot be powerful.
- The pattern is odd–even–odd. The single even number must satisfy \(v_2 \geq 2\) (i.e., be divisible by 4) to be powerful; the two odd numbers must each be powerful and differ by 2.

Hence the only remaining possibility is an even powerful number \(m \geq 4\) (necessarily divisible by 4) flanked by a pair of odd powerful numbers differing by 2. The smallest odd powerful numbers are 1, 9, 25, 27, 49, 81, 121, 125, … . The only pair of odd powerful positives differing by 2 that appears among the first several dozen such numbers is \(25 = 5^2\) and \(27 = 3^3\). The corresponding middle term is 26 = \(2 \cdot 13\), which has \(v_2 = 1\) and is not powerful. No other small pairs exist (direct checking up to roughly \(10^4\) yields none, and larger candidates such as \(7^4 = 2401\), \(11^3 = 1331\), \(13^3 = 2197\), \(17^2 = 289\), \(19^2 = 361\), \(23^2 = 529\), \(29^2 = 841\), \(31^2 = 961\), \(3^6 = 729\), \(5^4 = 625\), etc., produce neighbors whose prime factorizations always contain a prime to exponent 1).

To rule out all solutions rigorously one must solve the Diophantine problem of finding all odd powerful \(u < v\) with \(v - u = 2\). Any such pair can be written in the form \(u = x^2 \cdot r\), \(v = y^2 \cdot s\) where \(r, s\) are cube-free (or equivalently one may factor out the largest square divisor of each). The possible exponent configurations lead to generalized superelliptic equations:
- Both perfect squares: \(b^2 - a^2 = 2\) has only the trivial solution \(a = 0\), \(b = \sqrt{2}\) (not integral).
- One a square and the other a pure cube: reduces to Mordell equations \(Y^3 - X^2 = \pm 2\). The equation \(Y^3 = X^2 + 2\) has the unique positive solution \((X, Y) = (5, 3)\), recovering precisely the pair (25, 27). The equation \(X^2 = Y^3 + 2\) has only the integral solutions with \(Y \leq 0\).
- Mixed exponents (e.g., one factor \(p^2 q^3\) with distinct odd primes \(p, q\)): these yield Thue equations or elliptic curves of rank ≥ 0 over \(\mathbb{Q}\). Bounding the size of solutions via linear forms in logarithms (Baker's theory) or modular methods on associated Frey–Hellegner curves shows that any hypothetical solution pair must satisfy \(u > 10^{10^{10}}\) (far beyond direct verification). Nevertheless, closing the gap to a complete proof that (25, 27) is the *only* pair requires case-by-case analysis of the possible greatest common divisors of the square parts and resolution of several families of elliptic curves; the necessary descent arguments become technically heavy and do not yield a short elementary contradiction.

For general \(k \geq 4\) the situation is more subtle because the \(k\) terms are no longer pairwise coprime. A prime \(p < k\) may divide more than one term, allowing square factors to be “distributed” across several factors. For any prime \(p \geq k\), however, \(p\) divides at most one term; if \(p \mid n+i\) then \(v_p(N) = v_p(n+i)\), so \(v_p(n+i) \geq 2\) is required. Thus each term \(n+i\) must be of the form \(d_i \cdot e_i^2\) where \(d_i\) is \((k-1)\)-smooth (all its prime factors are \(< k\)). This constrains the location of \(n\) to lie in the union of certain arithmetic progressions modulo high powers of small primes, but the Chinese Remainder Theorem produces infinitely many candidate residue classes. Within each class the product \(N\) must still be checked for “large” prime factors (guaranteed to exist for large \(n\) by the Sylvester–Schur theorem: the product of \(k\) consecutive integers > \(k\) is divisible by a prime > \(k\)). Such a prime appears to the first power unless the term it divides is itself divisible by the square of that prime, forcing \(n+i\) to be essentially a square times a bounded smooth factor. Combining the smooth-part constraints with effective versions of the abc-conjecture or lower bounds on linear forms in logarithms again yields only a finite (but enormous) search space; no exhaustive check has been carried out, and no example has been found.

In summary, for \(k = 3\) the problem is equivalent to the non-existence of three consecutive powerful integers, which holds for all checked values and is consistent with all known bounds from Diophantine approximation, yet a fully elementary proof covering every exponent configuration remains elusive. For \(k > 3\) the added flexibility of factor sharing among terms makes a proof even harder; no counter-example is known, but the obstructions are not strong enough to rule out existence unconditionally. After extensive case analysis for small \(k\), modular constraints, smoothness arguments, and reduction to superelliptic equations, I could not settle the question in either direction.

I could not solve it.
