These are now usually called **crescent configurations**: (n) points in the plane in general position (no 3 collinear, no 4 concyclic) such that the (\binom n2) pairwise distances take only (n-1) distinct values, and the multiplicities of those values are exactly (1,2,\dots,n-1) (in some order). ([arXiv][1])

### What is known in (\mathbb R^2)

* **(n=1,2)**: trivial (vacuously satisfy the “no 3/no 4” conditions, and the distances/multiplicities condition is immediate).

* **(n=3)**: yes (any non-equilateral isosceles triangle gives multiplicities (2) and (1)).

* **(n=4)**: yes; a standard example is an isosceles triangle together with its center point (e.g. circumcenter), giving multiplicities (3,2,1). ([Erdős Problems][2])

* **(n=5)**: yes (construction due to Pomerance, described in Erdős’s problem discussions). ([Erdős Problems][2])

* **(n=6,7,8)**: yes; explicit planar examples exist. In particular Palásti exhibited a configuration for **(n=8)** (and related constructions for smaller (n)). ([Erdős Problems][2])

So, **crescent configurations are known to exist in the plane for all (n\le 8)** (and trivially for (n=1,2)). ([Erdős Problems][2])

### What is not known

* For **(n\ge 9)**: **no planar construction is currently known**, and the existence question is **open**. ([arXiv][3])
  (There have even been computer searches in regions of the triangular lattice that found no (n=9) example there, but that does not rule out existence in general.) ([Williams College][4])

Erdős conjectured that **for sufficiently large (n)** such configurations should **not** exist in the plane, but this remains unproved. ([arXiv][3])

**Answer (best current):** they are known for (n=1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8); for (n\ge 9) it is **unknown**. ([Erdős Problems][2])
