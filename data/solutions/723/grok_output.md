The problem asks whether the existence of a (finite) projective plane of order \(n\) forces \(n\) to be a prime power. While planes of order \(n = p^k\) (for prime \(p\)) are known to exist via the construction over the finite field \(\mathrm{GF}(p^k)\), and while nonexistence has been established for many specific composite \(n\) not a prime power (e.g., via the Bruck–Ryser theorem when \(n \equiv 1,2 \pmod{4}\) and \(n\) is not a sum of two integer squares, or via computer search for small cases like \(n=10\)), no general proof is known that rules out all such \(n\). The smallest unsettled composite orders not excluded by existing theorems include cases like \(n=12\) (though even here, partial constraints exist from other necessary conditions on the parameters). Thus the statement remains open in general.
