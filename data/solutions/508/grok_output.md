# The Chromatic Number of the Plane

The chromatic number of the plane \(\chi(\mathbb{R}^2)\) is defined to be the smallest positive integer \(k\) such that \(\mathbb{R}^2\) admits a coloring with \(k\) colors in which no two points at Euclidean distance exactly 1 receive the same color. Equivalently, it is the chromatic number of the infinite unit-distance graph with vertex set \(\mathbb{R}^2\) and edges between all pairs at distance 1.

This quantity is unknown, but tight bounds can be derived by explicit constructions: a finite unit-distance subgraph of the plane that forces at least a certain number of colors, and an explicit periodic coloring of \(\mathbb{R}^2\) that uses a fixed number of colors while avoiding monochromatic unit distances. We derive the bounds \(4 \leq \chi(\mathbb{R}^2) \leq 7\) below. (Stronger lower bounds exist via more elaborate constructions, but the argument for 4 already illustrates the method.)

## Lower Bound: \(\chi(\mathbb{R}^2) \geq 4\)

It suffices to exhibit a finite set of points in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) together with all pairs at distance exactly 1 (a unit-distance graph) whose chromatic number is 4. The *Moser spindle* provides such an example; we construct it explicitly and analyze its colorings.

Identify the plane with \(\mathbb{C}\) for convenience. Fix a rhombus with side length 1 and angles \(60^\circ\) and \(120^\circ\). Its vertices can be placed as
\[
0, \quad 1, \quad 1 + e^{i\pi/3}, \quad e^{i\pi/3}.
\]
All sides have length 1. The short diagonal (connecting the \(120^\circ\) vertices) also has length 1, while the long diagonal has length \(\sqrt{3}\). The two acute-angle (\(60^\circ\)) vertices can share a color in any proper 3-coloring of this rhombus, while the obtuse-angle vertices must receive the other two colors.

To force a fourth color, take two copies of this rhombus, sharing one acute vertex at the origin, and rotate the second copy by an angle \(\theta = \arccos(5/7)\) (approximately \(38.213^\circ\), but the exact algebraic value is chosen so the free acute vertices lie at distance exactly 1). The resulting seven-point configuration (the Moser spindle) has 11 unit-distance edges.

Suppose for contradiction there is a proper 3-coloring with colors \(\{A, B, C\}\). Without loss of generality, the shared acute vertex at the origin receives color \(A\). In each rhombus copy, the two obtuse vertices must then receive \(B\) and \(C\) (in some order), forcing each free acute vertex to receive color \(A\) (the only remaining option compatible with its two neighbors at distance 1). But these two free acute vertices are at distance 1 by construction of \(\theta\), so they cannot both receive \(A\): a contradiction. Thus, the Moser spindle requires 4 colors. Since it embeds in the plane with only unit distances as edges, \(\chi(\mathbb{R}^2) \geq 4\).

## Upper Bound: \(\chi(\mathbb{R}^2) \leq 7\)

We explicitly partition \(\mathbb{R}^2\) into 7 sets, each free of unit distances. Consider a tiling of the plane by regular hexagons with side length \(a = 0.4\). (Any \(a < 1/2\) works after adjusting the lattice scale below; we pick a concrete value for explicit verification.)

- The *diameter* of each such hexagon (maximum distance between any two points in it) is \(2a = 0.8 < 1\). Thus, each hexagon is free of unit distances internally and may receive a single color.
- The centers of adjacent hexagons in the tiling form a triangular lattice with minimal spacing \(d = a\sqrt{3} \approx 0.6928\).
- Color the hexagons periodically using a supercell consisting of 7 small hexagons (a central hexagon surrounded by a ring of 6). This corresponds to coloring the triangular lattice of centers by the rule \((m + 2n) \mod 7\), where \(m, n \in \mathbb{Z}\) are coordinates in the basis \(\{ (d, 0), (d/2, d\sqrt{3}/2) \}\). The full tiling of the plane is obtained by translating this supercell on the lattice generated by vectors of length \(d\sqrt{7}\) in appropriate directions (specifically, the minimal translation vectors preserving the coloring are of length \(d\sqrt{7} \approx 1.833\)).

Now consider any two distinct hexagons of the *same* color. Their centers are at least \(d\sqrt{7} \approx 1.833\) apart. Each hexagon has circumradius \(a = 0.4\), so the infimum distance between a point in one and a point in the other is at least
\[
d\sqrt{7} - 2a \approx 1.833 - 0.8 = 1.033 > 1.
\]
Thus, no two points in distinct same-color hexagons can be at distance exactly 1. Combined with the diameter bound within each hexagon, each color class is free of unit distances.

This yields a well-defined 7-coloring of \(\mathbb{R}^2\) (assign to each point the color of its containing hexagon; boundaries have measure zero and can be assigned arbitrarily without introducing unit distances, as the strict inequalities above allow infinitesimal perturbation). Hence, \(\chi(\mathbb{R}^2) \leq 7\).

## Conclusion

The constructions show \(4 \leq \chi(\mathbb{R}^2) \leq 7\). The exact value is unknown (it could be 4, 5, 6, or 7), as no unit-distance subgraph forcing 8 colors is known, nor is any coloring with 3 colors. Refinements (e.g., replacing hexagons by other tiles or optimizing the lattice) do not improve the upper bound, and tighter lower bounds require substantially larger unit-distance graphs, but the gap has not been closed.

**Final answer:** unknown (between 4 and 7)
