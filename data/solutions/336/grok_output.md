The problem asks for the value of \(\lim_{r \to \infty} \frac{h(r)}{r^2}\), where \(h(r)\) (for \(r \geq 2\)) is the largest finite \(k\) such that there exists a set \(A \subseteq \mathbb{N}\) with the following two properties:

- Every sufficiently large integer has a representation as a sum of at most \(r\) (not necessarily distinct) elements of \(A\).
- Every sufficiently large integer has a representation as a sum of exactly \(k\) (not necessarily distinct) elements of \(A\).

To determine the limit, one must first establish that \(h(r) < \infty\) for each fixed \(r \geq 2\), then determine the growth rate of \(h(r)\) precisely enough to extract the limit (if it exists).

Consider an arbitrary candidate set \(A\). Let \(s = \min A \geq 1\). Any sum of exactly \(k\) elements from \(A\) is at least \(ks\). Thus, if there exists some \(N\) such that all \(n > N\) are sums of exactly \(k\) elements from \(A\), it must be that \(N \geq ks\) (otherwise the integers in \((N, ks)\) provide immediate counterexamples). This already constrains \(k\) from above in terms of \(N\), but \(N\) itself depends on \(A\) and can be chosen after the fact, so this bound is not immediately useful.

To have every large \(n\) as a sum of at most \(r\) elements from \(A\), \(A\) must be sufficiently dense: if \(A\) has arbitrarily large gaps, then (even allowing repetitions) there will be arbitrarily large integers that cannot be expressed as such a sum without using more than \(r\) terms from \(A\) to "bridge" the gaps. On the other hand, to have every large \(n\) as a sum of *exactly* \(k\) elements from \(A\), \(A\) must contain sufficiently many small elements (to allow fine adjustments for large \(k\), where the average summand size \(n/k\) is small) *and* sufficiently many large elements (to reach large \(n\) without exceeding exactly \(k\) terms). If \(A\) is too dense (e.g., cofinite), both conditions hold for all \(k \geq 1\), so there is no *maximal* finite \(k\). If \(A\) is too sparse (e.g., the powers of 2), the second condition fails for all \(k\), as the \(k\)-fold sumsets miss infinitely many integers (e.g., those with Hamming weight \(\neq k\)).

Thus, any \(A\) achieving a large finite \(k\) for a given \(r\) must carefully balance density: dense enough at all scales for the "at most \(r\)" condition to hold, but sparse enough in some arithmetic progressions (or with controlled gaps) so that for all \(k'\) larger than some threshold, the exactly-\(k'\) sums miss infinitely many residue classes or have persistently large gaps. A natural candidate construction is to fix a modulus \(m\) (chosen large with \(r\)) and let \(A\) contain all sufficiently large integers from a carefully chosen subset \(S \subseteq \{0, 1, \dots, m-1\}\) with \(\gcd(S, m) = 1\), plus finitely many exceptions if needed to handle small values. The conditions then reduce to the sumsets in \(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}\):

- \(\bigcup_{j=1}^r jS = \mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}\) (where \(jS\) denotes the \(j\)-fold sumset allowing repetitions),
- \(kS = \mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}\).

For large enough elements of \(A\), the modular condition ensures no periodic gaps in the sums, and one can check directly that the non-periodic gaps are bounded (using that the elements of \(A\) eventually include full arithmetic progressions). However, for this \(S\), once the modular covering conditions hold for a given \(k\), they tend to hold for all larger \(k'\) (since adding more summands from a set generating \(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}\) eventually fills all residues if \(|S| \geq 2\)). This suggests \(h(r) = \infty\), contradicting that \(h(r)\) is defined to be finite. Adjusting by disallowing repetitions in sums leads to similar issues: taking \(|S| \approx m/r\) large allows the modular conditions to hold for \(k\) up to \(\Theta(m)\), and \(m\) can be taken arbitrarily large.

Constructions with a finite "small" subset \(B \subset A\) (for adjustments, with \(|B|\) large to support large \(k\)) and an infinite "large" subset \(L = A \setminus B\) (an arithmetic progression with difference \(d > \sum B\), spaced to match the subset-sum adjustment ranges from \(B\)) encounter analogous difficulties. The "at most \(r\)" condition requires that the gap size in \(L\) is bounded in terms of \(r\) and the adjustment ranges achievable with at most \(r\) total terms (from \(B \cup L\)). The "exactly \(k\)" condition requires covering all large \(n\) using *precisely* \(k\) terms, which forces \(k \lesssim |B| + O(1)\) (since using too many elements from \(L\) makes the sum too large on average unless elements of \(L\) are small, contradicting that they are large).
