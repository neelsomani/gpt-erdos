The angular distribution of the roots \(z_1, \dots, z_d\) of \(f\) is equivalent (after multiplying \(f\) by a suitable unimodular constant, which does not change \(n\) or \(M\)) to studying the arguments \(\theta_j = \arg(z_j)\) after a possible global rotation of all roots (which corresponds to the substitution \(x \mapsto x e^{i\phi}\) and again leaves \(n\) and \(M\) invariant). Thus, without loss of generality, it suffices to fix an arbitrary interval \(I = [\alpha, \alpha + \beta] \subseteq [0, 2\pi]\) with \(0 < \beta \leq 2\pi\) and bound the deviation
\[
D(I) := \left| \#\{j : \theta_j \in I\} - \frac{\beta}{2\pi} d \right|.
\]
The goal is to show \(D(I) \ll \sqrt{n \log M}\) with an absolute implied constant, or to find a counterexample where this fails for some sequence of polynomials.

To count roots in a sector, apply the argument principle to a contour consisting of the rays at angles \(\alpha\) and \(\alpha + \beta\) (truncated at small radius \(\varepsilon > 0\) and large radius \(R > 0\)) together with the connecting circular arcs. For a root \(z_j = r_j e^{i\theta_j}\), we have \(\theta_j \in I\) if and only if the image under \(f\) winds in a manner contributing to the change in argument along this contour. However, the contributions from the inner and outer arcs must be estimated. On the arc \(|z| = R\) (with \(R\) larger than all \(|z_j|\)), \(f(z) \sim a_d z^d\), so the argument change is approximately \(d\beta\) plus an error controlled by
\[
\left| \frac{f'(z)}{f(z)} - \frac{d}{z} \right| \leq \frac{C \sum_{k=0}^{d-1} |a_k| R^{k-1}}{|a_d| R^d - \sum_{k=0}^{d-1} |a_k| R^k}.
\]
An analogous estimate holds on \(|z| = \varepsilon\) (with the roles of \(a_0\) and \(a_d\) reversed). Choosing \(\varepsilon\) smaller than all \(|z_j|\) and \(R\) larger than all \(|z_j|\), and using the definition of \(M\), these errors are at most \(O(\log M)\) in total after integration (the logarithmic derivative contributes a factor of the relative coefficient sizes). This reduces the problem to controlling
\[
\frac{1}{2\pi i} \int_{\gamma} \frac{f'(z)}{f(z)} \, dz,
\]
where \(\gamma\) is the truncated sector boundary, but the radial integrals remain.

The radial integrals are difficult to bound directly because the moduli \(r_j\) may vary over exponentially many scales. To handle this, group the roots into dyadic annuli \(2^\ell \leq |z_j| < 2^{\ell+1}\) for \(\ell \in \mathbb{Z}\). Within each annulus, rescale by \(2^{-\ell}\) so that the roots lie in an annulus of bounded aspect ratio. The contribution to \(D(I)\) from each annulus is then at most the number of roots in that annulus whose arguments lie in \(I\), and the total deviation is the sum over \(O(\log M)\) annuli (since the product of all \(|z_j|\) is \(|a_0/a_d|\), so the sum of \(\log r_j\) is controlled by \(\log M\)). However, this only yields the trivial bound \(D(I) \leq d\), and to improve it one must exploit sparsity within each annulus.

Within a fixed annulus, the rescaled polynomial has the same \(n\) (at most), but the effective coefficients in the annulus are obtained by domination: for roots with \(|z| \approx 1\) (after rescaling), the term \(a_k z^k\) has size roughly \(|a_k|\). By the triangle inequality on the unit circle (after rescaling), \(|f(e^{i\theta})|\) can be bounded below on average by \(\exp(-O(\log M))\) outside \(O(n)\) "bad" arcs whose total measure is \(O(n \log M / d)\) (using Jensen's formula on \(\log |f|\) and the fact that the \(L^1\) norm of \(\log |f(re^{i\theta})|\) is at most \(\log M + O(1)\) for \(r \approx 1\)). The number of roots in \(I\) is then comparable to the winding contribution, which by the above is controlled by the measure of the bad arcs intersecting \(I\).

This yields \(D(I) \ll n + \sqrt{n \log M}\) in each annulus (the linear term in \(n\) arising from the number of bad arcs, and the square-root term from square-root cancellation in the variance of the integrated argument change, as the Fourier coefficients of \(\log |f(e^{i\theta})|\) vanish except at the \(n\) frequencies corresponding to the nonzero terms). Summing over the \(O(\log M)\) annuli multiplies the error by \(\sqrt{\log M}\), producing an overall bound \(D(I) \ll n\sqrt{\log M} + \sqrt{n} \log M\), which is worse than the claimed \(\sqrt{n \log M}\) (the extra factors of \(n\) and \(\sqrt{\log M}\) arise from the dyadic decomposition and from crude estimates on the arcs).

To remove the excess factors, one would need a more refined decomposition (e.g., a wavelet decomposition in angle and radius simultaneously, or a direct application of discrepancy inequalities in the toral direction after suitable conformal mapping of the sector), combined with \(L^2\) mean estimates on the derivative of \(\arg f(re^{i\theta})\) that exploit sparsity more sharply than the \(L^1\) bounds from Jensen. Such a refinement appears plausible but requires controlling the cross terms between different annuli (which contribute to the same angular interval \(I\)) without inflating the error by more than a constant factor. No such sharpening is immediate, and the dyadic decomposition as above cannot be improved in an elementary way without additional input on the correlations between scales.

A disproof by counterexample is likewise elusive. Polynomials with \(n\) terms cannot have more than \(O(n)\) roots exactly on any fixed ray (a generalization of Descartes' rule of signs holds for complex coefficients via considering the Newton polygon in the tropical limit, which has at most \(n-1\) slopes). Thus, for very small \(|I|\), \(D(I) \leq O(n) + O(|I|d)\). When \(d \gg n\), this is consistent with the claimed bound only if \(M \gg \exp(cn)\) (forcing large coefficient imbalance to permit angular clustering), but constructing high-degree examples with \(n = 3\) (say) and \(D(I) \gg \sqrt{\log M}\) for some \(I\) of length \(\approx 1/\sqrt{\log M}\) requires solving a system of \(d\) equations in the coefficients and root positions simultaneously, which overconstrains the system and appears impossible. Random sparse polynomials (nonzero coefficients chosen with random phases and log-normal magnitudes) yield angular equidistribution with high probability and deviation \(O(\sqrt{n \log M})\) by standard concentration, consistent with the claimed bound but not a proof for the worst case.
