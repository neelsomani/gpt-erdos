# Grok Response for Problem 826

**I could not solve it.**

The problem asks whether there are infinitely many positive integers \(n\) such that \(\tau(n+k) \ll k\) holds for every integer \(k \geq 1\), where \(\tau\) denotes the divisor function. Interpreting \(\ll\) in the standard sense (i.e., the existence of an absolute constant \(C > 0\) independent of both \(n\) and \(k\) such that \(\tau(n+k) \leq C \cdot k\) for all \(k \geq 1\)), the statement is equivalent to asking whether
\[
\liminf_{n \to \infty} \sup_{k \geq 1} \frac{\tau(n+k)}{k} < \infty.
\]
(If the implied constant is permitted to depend on \(n\), the claim holds for *all* \(n\), since \(\tau(m) \ll_\varepsilon m^\varepsilon\) for any \(\varepsilon > 0\) implies that the supremum is finite for each fixed \(n\); this renders the problem trivial, which is inconsistent with its appearance in a list of Erdős problems.)

To decide the claim, one must determine whether the union of “forbidden” intervals preceding each \(m \geq 2\) covers all sufficiently large integers. Specifically, for a candidate constant \(C\), the integer \(m\) forbids the predecessors \(n = m - k\) for all \(1 \leq k \leq \lfloor \tau(m)/C \rfloor\). Only those \(m\) with \(\tau(m) > C\) forbid at least the immediate predecessor \(m-1\); since the normal order of \(\tau(m)\) is \(\exp((\log 2 + o(1))\log\log m)\), for any fixed \(C\) one has \(\tau(m) > C\) for almost all \(m\). Thus almost all \(n\) are forbidden at \(k=1\), but the exceptional set (where \(\tau(n+1) \leq C\)) is infinite—for instance, it contains all \(n = p-1\) with \(p\) prime.

Restricting to such exceptional \(n = p-1\) (so that the \(k=1\) condition holds for \(C \geq 2\)), the remaining task is to ensure that no \(m = n+k\) with \(2 \leq k \leq D(n)\) satisfies \(\tau(m) > Ck\), where \(D(n) \asymp \exp((\log 2 + \varepsilon)\log n / \log\log n)\) exceeds the maximal order of \(\tau\) up to \(n + D(n)\). For \(k > D(n)\) the maximal-order bound on \(\tau\) already yields \(\tau(n+k) < Ck\) automatically. The highly divisible \(m\) (those with \(\tau(m)\) near the maximal order) are sparse: their counting function up to \(x\) is at most \(x^{o(1)}\) for the relevant range of \(\tau(m)\), and the gaps between them typically exceed \(D(n)\). Each such \(m\) forbids an interval of length at most \(D(n)\) preceding it, poisoning at most \(D(n)/\log n = n^{o(1)}\) primes in that interval. Summing over the \(n^{o(1)}\) relevant \(m \leq 2n\) suggests that only \(n^{o(1)}\) primes are poisoned, leaving infinitely many unpoisoned primes \(p\) (hence infinitely many good \(n = p-1\)) provided the \(o(1)\) exponents can be made strictly less than 1 uniformly.

While the preceding counting and gap argument is suggestive and indicates that the liminf is likely finite for sufficiently large \(C\), converting the heuristics into a rigorous proof requires uniform upper bounds on the counting function of \(\{m \leq x : \tau(m) \geq y\}\) (with \(y\) near the maximal order), effective gap estimates between such \(m\), and a sieve to produce infinitely many primes \(p\) avoiding all forbidden intervals of length \(D(p)\) simultaneously with the small-\(k\) conditions \(\tau(p+k) \leq Ck\) for \(k \ll (\log p)^{O(1)}\). These ingredients do not appear to follow from standard estimates on \(\tau\) (e.g., the maximal-order theorem of Wigert–Ramanujan or the normal-order theorem of Erdős–Kac) without additional input. Attempts to close the gaps via the Chinese Remainder Theorem (to control small \(k\)) or zero-density estimates likewise encounter obstacles connected to the irregular distribution of smooth or highly divisible integers in short intervals.

Consequently, I could not settle whether the liminf is finite.
