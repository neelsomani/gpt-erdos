**Reasoning.** Let \(d_{\min}(A) = 1\) denote the minimum pairwise distance in \(A\), and let \(\operatorname{diam}(A)\) be its diameter. Let \(D(n)\) be the infimum of \(\operatorname{diam}(A)\) over all such \(n\)-point sets. The problem asks whether there exists \(N\) such that any \(A\) achieving (or arbitrarily close to) \(D(n)\) for \(n \geq N\) must contain three points at mutual distance exactly 1.

The triangular lattice \(\Lambda\) (with minimum distance 1) has density \(2/\sqrt{3}\) (area \(\sqrt{3}/2\) per point) and realizes many equilateral triangles of side 1. Finite subsets of \(\Lambda\) (e.g., hexagonal or "rounded" pieces) achieve \(\operatorname{diam}(A) \asymp c \sqrt{n}\) with leading constant determined by this density. Standard area arguments (any set of diameter \(D\) is contained in a region of area \(O(D^2)\)) show that \(D(n) \gtrsim \sqrt{n \sqrt{3}/2}\) asymptotically, so such subsets are near-optimal for large \(n\).

Now suppose \(A\) has \(d_{\min}(A) = 1\) but no equilateral triangle of side 1. Let \(G\) be the *contact graph* on \(A\), with an edge between \(p, q \in A\) if and only if \(\|p - q\| = 1\). This graph is planar: edges can be drawn as straight lines (or curves through tangency points of radius-\(1/2\) disks) without crossings. Absence of equilateral triangles of side 1 means \(G\) is triangle-free. Moreover, at any vertex the angular separation between incident edges is strictly greater than \(60^\circ\) (equality would force a third edge of length 1 by the law of cosines, creating a triangle in \(G\); angles \(< 60^\circ\) would violate \(d_{\min}(A) = 1\)). Thus \(\Delta(G) \leq 5\).

Since \(G\) is planar and triangle-free, Euler's formula implies \(e(G) \leq 2n - 4\) (for \(n \geq 3\)), so the average degree is strictly less than 4. (In the infinite limit this bound is sharp, but finite packings cannot saturate it.) This already suggests "wasted space" relative to \(\Lambda\) (average degree 6).

To obtain a matching upper bound on density, consider the lattice \(\Lambda_\theta\) generated by vectors of length 1 with included angle \(\theta > 60^\circ\). The minimum distance is exactly 1 (the next-nearest distance is \(2\sin(\theta/2) > 1\)), the contact graph is 4-regular (a distorted integer lattice, triangle-free), and the area per point is \(\sin \theta > \sqrt{3}/2\). The density \(\pi/(4 \sin \theta)\) approaches that of \(\Lambda\) from below as \(\theta \to 60^\circ+\). Finite "rounded" subsets of \(\Lambda_\theta\) (chosen to minimize Euclidean diameter for fixed \(n\)) thus achieve diameters arbitrarily close to the lower bound above, but always strictly larger than those from subsets of \(\Lambda\) (for fixed \(n\)).

The gap can be made arbitrarily small by taking \(\theta\) sufficiently close to \(60^\circ\), but it is positive for each fixed \(n\). Whether this forces \(\operatorname{diam}(A) > D(n)\) (i.e., whether near-optimal configurations *without* side-1 equilateral triangles exist, or whether discreteness and boundary effects in finite near-optimal packings force at least one such triangle) appears to depend on fine-scale geometry of optimal packings. No rigorous proof that the gap is uniformly bounded away from zero (for all large \(n\)) or explicit construction closing the gap without introducing a triangle was found. Small-\(n\) cases (e.g., \(n=3\) forces an equilateral triangle to achieve \(D(3)=1\); \(n=4,5\) can be checked by enumeration) do not obviously extend.

Thus the statement is plausible for large \(n\) (optimal configurations must locally resemble \(\Lambda\)) but unresolved.
