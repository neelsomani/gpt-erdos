# Grok Response for Problem 653

The function \(R(x_i)\) counts the number of distinct Euclidean distances from \(x_i\) to the remaining \(n-1\) points. Thus, each \(R(x_i)\) is an integer in \(\{1, 2, \dots, n-1\}\), and \(g(n)\) is the largest cardinality of the image \(\{R(x) : x \in S\}\) over all \(n\)-element \(S \subset \mathbb{R}^2\). The claim is equivalent to asking whether there exist configurations \(S_n\) (one for each large \(n\)) such that \(|\{R(x) : x \in S_n\}| \geq (1 - o(1))n\).

To approach the claim from below, fix a point \(c \in S\) (to be a "center") and distribute the remaining \(n-1\) points onto \(k\) circles centered at \(c\), for a parameter \(k\) to be chosen later (with the radii distinct and positive). This forces \(R(c) = k\). Now consider an arbitrary \(y \neq c\). The distance from \(y\) to \(c\) contributes one value to \(R(y)\). The remaining \(n-2\) points lie on the \(k\) circles around \(c\). Any circle centered at \(y\) intersects each of these \(k\) circles in at most two points, so at most \(2k\) of the \(n-2\) points can realize any fixed distance from \(y\) (ignoring the measure-zero case in which this distance equals \(|y - c|\) and incorporates \(c\) itself). It follows that
\[
R(y) \geq 1 + \left\lceil \frac{n-2}{2k} \right\rceil = \Omega\left(\frac{n}{k}\right),
\]
with the implicit constant at least \(1/2\).

Thus, in this configuration, one value (\(k\)) is realized, while the other \(n-1\) values of \(R\) are all at least \(\Omega(n/k)\). The largest possible value of \(R\) is \(n-1\) (realized if all distances from a point are distinct). Suppose parameters and positions on the circles can be chosen so that every integer in \([\Omega(n/k), n-1]\) is realized at least once as \(R(y)\) for some \(y \neq c\). (This requires sufficiently many degrees of freedom in the configuration---approximately \(n + k\) after fixing the position of \(c\), coming from the choice of radii and angular positions---to impose enough independent algebraic conditions \(|y - a| = |y - b|\) to force exact collisions in distances from chosen \(y\)'s, without forcing unintended collisions that would skip values or collapse multiple \(R(y)\)'s together. Each such condition is a hypersurface in the configuration space, and for \(o(n)\) total conditions the complement is nonempty.)

The number of distinct integers realized is then at least
\[
(n-1) - \Omega\left(\frac{n}{k}\right) + 1
\]
(if \(k \leq \Omega(n/k)\), so that the isolated value \(k\) does not create an additional gap) plus the isolated value \(k\) itself if it lies outside \([\Omega(n/k), n-1]\). Setting \(k = \Theta(\sqrt{n})\) makes the lower bound \(\Omega(\sqrt{n})\) and yields at least \(n - O(\sqrt{n})\) distinct values, which is \((1 - o(1))n\).

The same counting shows that choosing \(k = 1\) (all \(n-1\) points on a single circle) forces all peripheral \(R(y) \geq 1 + \lceil (n-2)/2 \rceil \approx n/2\), so at best the realized values are \(\{1\} \cup [\approx n/2, n-1]\), again \(n/2 + O(1)\) many if all integers in the upper interval are hit. The arithmetic progression on a line realizes all integers from \(\lceil (n-1)/2 \rceil\) to \(n-1\) (exactly \(\approx n/2\) many), consistent with viewing a line as a degenerate case of two circles of infinite radius (at most one point per "circle" per distance, doubling the denominator in the \(\Omega(n/k)\) bound).

However, saturating the lower bound \(\Omega(n/k)\) for a given \(y\) requires nearly maximal multiplicity \(2k\) on most distance circles around \(y\), which in turn requires the point set to be symmetric with respect to the line through \(c\) and \(y\) (to occupy both intersection points on each circle around \(c\)). Imposing this symmetry for even one \(y\) consumes \(\Theta(n)\) degrees of freedom, and imposing it for multiple \(y\)'s forces the set toward regularity (e.g., vertices of regular polygons or lattices), in which case most or all peripheral points realize the same \(R(y)\). Even without saturating the minimum, reducing \(R(y)\) from \(n-1\) by even a few integers for many distinct \(y\) requires many independent distance-collision conditions. These conditions are nonlocal (each involves a specific \(y\) and pairs of other points) and likely to interact, either forcing extra collisions (skipping integers in the image of \(R\)) or violating the genericity needed to hit every integer in a long interval like \([\Omega(\sqrt{n}), n-1]\).

No construction is known that rigorously hits \(\omega(n)\) consecutive integers in the image of \(R\) while keeping the lower end of the image as small as \(o(n)\). Thus the above only heuristically suggests \(g(n) \geq (1 - o(1))n\) (via a sufficiently flexible choice of \(k = \Theta(\sqrt{n})\) and sufficiently independent collisions), but does not prove it. An opposing heuristic is that symmetries needed for small \(R(y)\) tend to equalize the \(R\)-values of other points, limiting the number of distinct realizations to \(O(n/\log n)\) or similar in explicit examples (e.g., subsets of lattices or unions of regular polygons). Upper bounds on \(g(n)\) are likewise elusive, as no rigidity or incidence theorem appears to force more than \(o(n)\) repetitions in the multiset \(\{R(x) : x \in S\}\).
