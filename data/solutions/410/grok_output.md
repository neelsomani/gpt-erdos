**Problem 410**

The sequence is defined by \(m_0 = n \geq 2\) and \(m_{k+1} = \sigma(m_k)\), where \(\sigma\) denotes the sum-of-divisors function. The question is whether
\[
\lim_{k \to \infty} m_k^{1/k} = \infty.
\]
Equivalently, for every \(C > 1\), does there exist \(K\) such that \(m_k > C^k\) for all \(k > K\)?

First, \(m_{k+1} > m_k\) for all \(k\), since \(\sigma(m) \geq m + 1 > m\) for every integer \(m \geq 1\). Thus \(m_k \to \infty\).

To bound the growth from above, recall that
\[
\limsup_{m \to \infty} \frac{\sigma(m)}{m \log \log m} = e^\gamma
\]
(Gronwall's theorem), so there exists an absolute constant \(C > 0\) such that
\[
\sigma(m) < C \, m \log \log m
\]
for all \(m \geq 3\). Let \(l_k = \log m_k\). Then
\[
l_{k+1} < l_k + \log l_k + O(1).
\]
Treating this as the differential inequality \(\frac{dl}{dk} \leq \log l + O(1)\) and solving the associated ODE \(\frac{dl}{dk} = \log l\) by the substitution \(u = \log l\) yields
\[
\int \frac{e^u}{u} \, du \approx k,
\]
or \(k \sim \frac{e^u}{u}\). Thus \(u \sim \log k + \log \log k + o(1)\), so
\[
l_k \ll k \log k, \qquad m_k \ll \exp(O(k \log k)).
\]
Raising to the power \(1/k\),
\[
m_k^{1/k} \ll \exp(O(\log k)) = k^{O(1)}.
\]
This shows that \(m_k^{1/k}\) cannot grow faster than any positive power of \(k\).

For a lower bound, separate cases according to the form of \(m_k\).

- If \(m_k\) is an odd prime \(p\), then \(m_{k+1} = p + 1\) (even) and \(m_{k+2} = \sigma(p + 1)\). For even \(m > 2\),
  \[
  \frac{\sigma(m)}{m} > \frac{3}{2},
  \]
  with the infimum approached when \(m = 2p\) for large odd prime \(p\). Hence after two steps the value has grown by a factor strictly larger than \(3/2\). If infinitely many terms are odd primes, then on average
  \[
  m_{k+2} > \Bigl(\frac{3}{2}\Bigr) m_k,
  \]
  implying \(m_k \gg c^k\) for some \(c > 1\) and therefore \(\liminf m_k^{1/k} > 1\).

- If \(m_k > 1\) is odd and composite, it has a prime factor \(p \leq \sqrt{m_k}\), so
  \[
  \sigma(m_k) \geq m_k + p + 1 \geq m_k + \sqrt{m_k}.
  \]
  The recurrence \(m_{k+1} \geq m_k + \sqrt{m_k}\) integrates (via \(\frac{dm}{\sqrt{m}} \geq dk\)) to \(m_k \gg k^2\), whence
  \[
  m_k^{1/k} \geq (c k^2)^{1/k} = \exp\Bigl(\frac{O(\log k)}{k}\Bigr) \to 1.
  \]

- If \(m_k\) is even and greater than 2, the multiplicative factor \(\sigma(m_k)/m_k > 3/2\) as above, and the exact factor is at least \(7/4\) when \(m_k\) is a power of 2 at least 4, or larger when additional prime factors are present.

These lower bounds are compatible with \(m_k^{1/k}\) tending to a finite limit \(L > 1\) (if the geometric growth rate stabilizes around a fixed base >1) or with the limit equaling 1 (if sufficiently many terms are odd prime powers, forcing additive rather than multiplicative growth for long stretches). The upper-bound analysis is likewise compatible with a finite positive limit: if \(\limsup m_k^{1/k} = L < \infty\), then
\[
m_{k+1} < C m_k \log(k \log L),
\]
and
\[
m_{k+1}^{1/(k+1)} < L^{k/(k+1)} \cdot (C \log k)^{1/(k+1)} \to L,
\]
so no contradiction arises.

Because the sequence \((m_k)\) is deterministic, its prime factors are generated recursively from the prime factors of \(n\) via the cyclotomic polynomials implicit in \(\sigma(p^a) = 1 + p + \cdots + p^a\). When a large prime \(p\) divides some \(m_k\), the factor \(p + 1\) (or more generally \((p^{a+1} - 1)/(p - 1)\)) may introduce new large prime factors, keeping \(\sigma(m_k)/m_k\) arbitrarily close to 1 for arbitrarily large stretches. Conversely, repeated application tends to accumulate small prime factors (powers of 2, 3, etc.), which can force \(\sigma(m_k)/m_k\) to grow like \(\log \log m_k\) along subsequences. Deciding which behavior dominates—whether the average of \(\log(\sigma(m_k)/m_k)\) over \(k\) tends to 0 or to a positive constant or diverges—appears to require finer control over the arithmetic structure of the specific integers generated by iteration than is currently available.
