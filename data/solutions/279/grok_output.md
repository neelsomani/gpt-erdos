# Grok Response for Problem 279

**Problem 279**

Let \(k \geq 3\). We seek to determine whether there exist congruence classes \(a_p \pmod{p}\) (with \(0 \leq a_p < p\)) for each prime \(p\) such that all but finitely many positive integers \(n\) admit a representation
\[
n = a_p + t p
\]
for some prime \(p\) and some integer \(t \geq k\).

Equivalently, for all sufficiently large \(n\), there must exist a prime \(p \leq (n - a_p)/k\) (hence \(p \lesssim n/k\)) satisfying \(n \equiv a_p \pmod{p}\).

#### Attempted construction

Fix \(a_2 = 1\). Then every odd integer \(n \geq 1 + 2k\) satisfies \(n \equiv 1 \pmod{2}\) with
\[
t = \frac{n-1}{2} \geq k,
\]
so all sufficiently large odd \(n\) (prime, composite, or otherwise) are covered by \(p=2\).

It remains to cover all sufficiently large even \(n\). Set \(a_p = 0\) for all odd primes \(p\). Any even \(n\) that is not a power of 2 possesses at least one odd prime factor \(q\). Let \(q\) be the smallest such factor. Then \(q \leq \sqrt{n}\). For \(n > k^2\) we have \(\sqrt{n} < n/k\), so \(t = n/q \geq k\) and \(n \equiv 0 = a_q \pmod{q}\). Thus all even \(n\) that are not powers of 2 are covered for large \(n\).

The only remaining candidates for exceptions are large powers of 2, i.e., \(n = 2^m\) with \(m\) large. Such an \(n\) is even, so it is not covered by \(p=2\) (since \(2^m \equiv 0 \not\equiv 1 \pmod{2}\)). It is also not covered by any odd prime with \(a_p = 0\), because no odd prime divides \(2^m\). Coverage of \(2^m\) therefore requires an odd prime \(p \leq 2^m/k\) such that
\[
a_p \equiv 2^m \pmod{p}
\]
with the chosen (fixed) \(a_p \neq 0\).

For a fixed odd prime \(p\), the powers \(2^m \pmod{p}\) lie in the cyclic subgroup \(\langle 2 \rangle\) generated by 2 in \((\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^\times\), whose order is \(d = \operatorname{ord}_p(2)\) (necessarily dividing \(p-1\)). Choosing \(a_p \equiv 2^s \pmod{p}\) for some \(s\) causes \(p\) to cover precisely those exponents satisfying
\[
2^m \equiv 2^s \pmod{p} \iff d \mid (m-s) \iff m \equiv s \pmod{d}.
\]
Thus each such \(p\) (with \(a_p\) chosen in \(\langle 2 \rangle\)) covers exactly one arithmetic progression of exponents with difference \(d = \operatorname{ord}_p(2)\).

#### Obstacles encountered

- Setting \(a_p \neq 0\) for any odd prime \(p\) means that multiples of \(p\) are no longer covered by that \(p\) (since if \(p \mid n\) then \(n \equiv 0 \not\equiv a_p \pmod{p}\)). In particular, integers of the form \(2^a \cdot p^b\) (previously covered via \(a_p = 0\)) now require coverage by some other prime \(q \neq p,2\). This generates a new sparse but infinite set of exceptions (higher 2-\(p\)-smooth numbers) that must themselves be covered by yet further primes whose \(a_q\) are adjusted away from 0.
- Each newly adjusted prime covers only one arithmetic progression in the relevant exponent lattice (or, more generally, in the multiplicative semigroup generated by the small primes already “sacrificed”). Covering all remaining exceptional families therefore reduces to covering infinitely many sparse subsets of \(\mathbb{N}\) by unions of arithmetic progressions whose differences are orders of 2 modulo distinct primes. While Artin’s conjecture on primitive roots (if true) supplies primes with \(\operatorname{ord}_p(2) = p-1\) arbitrarily large, and while the exceptional sets are sparse, the adjustments propagate: each change disables coverage for an entire arithmetic progression of previously handled numbers, necessitating further primes and further adjustments.
- The self-referential constraint \(p \leq n/k\) is automatically satisfied for fixed \(p\) once \(n = 2^m\) (or any fixed-smooth number) is large, but the global choice of \(\{a_p\}\) must be made in advance and must simultaneously handle all such cascading exceptional families without leaving infinitely many uncovered integers.

#### Why a proof of existence or non-existence remains elusive

A probabilistic heuristic suggests that a random choice of \(a_p\) (uniform in \(\{0,1,\dots,p-1\}\)) leaves only a density-zero set uncovered: for a typical \(n\), the probability that \(n \not\equiv a_p \pmod{p}\) for all \(p \leq n/k\) is
\[
\prod_{p \leq n/k} \Bigl(1 - \frac{1}{p}\Bigr) \sim \frac{e^{-\gamma}}{\log(n/k)} \to 0
\]
as \(n \to \infty\). Mertens’ theorem supplies the asymptotic, but because the product is over a primorial modulus \(\exp(\theta(n/k)) \asymp \exp(n/k)\) (vastly larger than \(n\)), Dirichlet’s theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions cannot be applied directly inside intervals of length \(o(\exp(n/k))\) to locate concrete counterexamples. Consequently the heuristic does not yield a rigorous existence proof, nor does it produce an explicit construction that simultaneously neutralizes all smooth-number cascades.

Attempts to build an explicit covering by successively assigning \(a_p\) to handle successive smooth families (powers of 2, then \(2^a 3^b\), then \(2^a 3^b 5^c\), …) encounter the same difficulty: each new assignment disables coverage for an infinite arithmetic progression of previously covered integers, requiring an infinite regress of further primes. No finite or recursively enumerable choice of \(\{a_p\}\) has been shown to terminate with only finitely many exceptions.

After exhaustive examination of these constructions, density arguments, smooth-number cascades, and modular-order coverings, no proof that such a choice of \(\{a_p\}\) exists (nor that every choice leaves infinitely many exceptions) could be obtained.

Thus the problem remains unsolved.
