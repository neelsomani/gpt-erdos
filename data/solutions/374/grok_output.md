# Grok Response for Problem 374

**Problem 374.** For any \(m \in \mathbb{N}\), \(F(m)\) is the smallest integer \(k \geq 2\) (if it exists) such that there exist strictly increasing \(a_1 < \cdots < a_k = m\) with \(\prod_{i=1}^k a_i!\) a perfect square. Let \(D_k = \{m : F(m) = k\}\). The task is to determine the order of growth of \(|D_k \cap [1,n]|\) as \(n \to \infty\), for each fixed \(k\) with \(3 \leq k \leq 6\).

To analyze \(F(m)\), consider the vectors \(v(\ell) \in (\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{\pi(m)}\) for \(\ell \leq m\), where the coordinates are indexed by the primes \(p \leq m\) and the \(p\)-th coordinate of \(v(\ell)\) is the parity of the exponent of \(p\) in \(\ell!\) (given by \(\sum_{i \geq 1} \lfloor \ell / p^i \rfloor \mod 2\)). Then \(\prod_{i=1}^k a_i!\) is a square if and only if
\[
\sum_{i=1}^k v(a_i) = 0 \in (\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{\pi(m)}.
\]
Equivalently, \(F(m)\) is one more than the minimal number \(r \geq 1\) of distinct indices \(a < m\) such that \(v(m)\) equals the sum of the corresponding \(v(a)\)'s (if such an \(r\) exists). Thus \(m \in D_k\) if this minimal \(r = k-1\).

First consider the case \(k=2\) (though not asked). Here we require some \(a < m\) with \(v(a) = v(m)\). Since \(a < m\) implies \(a!\) divides \(m!\), we have
\[
a! \cdot m! = (a!)^2 \cdot \Bigl( \prod_{j=a+1}^m j \Bigr).
\]
This is a square if and only if \(\prod_{j=a+1}^m j\) is a square. By the Erdős–Selfridge theorem, a product of two or more consecutive positive integers is never a perfect power. Thus the product of \(\geq 2\) consecutive integers from \(a+1\) to \(m\) (\(m \geq a+2\)) is never a square. The only remaining case is \(a = m-1\), where the product is the single term \(m\), which is a square precisely when \(m\) is a square. Hence \(F(m) = 2\) if and only if \(m \geq 4\) is a perfect square (for \(m=1\) the condition \(k \geq 2\) cannot hold). In particular, \(|D_2 \cap [1,n]| \asymp \sqrt{n}\).

For \(k \geq 3\) the situation is more involved, as there is no direct reduction to a product of consecutive integers being a square times a factorial. Nevertheless, parity conditions on large primes yield strong constraints. Let \(p\) be a prime satisfying \(m/2 < p < m\) (such primes exist for all \(m \geq 4\) by Bertrand's postulate). The exponent of \(p\) in \(\ell!\) is \(0\) for all \(\ell < p\) and equals \(1 \mod 2\) for \(p \leq \ell < 2p\) (higher powers \(p^i\) with \(i \geq 2\) satisfy \(p^i > m\) for large enough \(p\)). Thus in the product \(\prod a_i!\), the total exponent of \(p\) equals (mod \(2\)) the number \(\nu(p)\) of selected \(a_i \geq p\). For the product to be a square we need \(\nu(p) \equiv 0 \pmod{2}\).

Since \(m > p\) is always selected, \(\nu(p) = 1 +\) (number selected in \([p,m)\)). Hence \(\nu(p)\) even if and only if the number selected in \([p,m)\) is *odd*. Each such large prime \(p\) therefore imposes an independent linear condition over \(\mathbb{GF}(2)\) on the selected indices in \((m/2,m)\): the parity of the number chosen in each nested interval \([p,m)\) must be odd. There are \(\sim m/(2 \log m)\) such primes \(p \in (m/2,m)\) on average. These conditions are nested (larger \(p\) give strictly smaller intervals \([p,m)\)), so they form a chain of cumulative parity conditions.

For fixed \(k\), only \(r = k-1\) indices \(< m\) may be selected in total. To affect the conditions for primes \(> m/2\), the selected indices must themselves lie in \((m/2,m)\); selections below \(m/2\) cannot flip any of these parities. Thus at most \(r \leq 5\) selections (for \(k \leq 6\)) are available to satisfy \(\sim m/(2\log m)\) nested parity conditions. When the primes in \((m/2,m)\) have typical gaps of size \(\sim \log m\), each "isolated" prime requires a dedicated selection in its interval to flip the parity, exhausting the budget of \(r\) selections for \(\gg 1\) such primes. Only special \(m\) with atypical prime configurations near \(m\) (e.g., large gaps or specific clusterings allowing a few selections to satisfy multiple conditions simultaneously, or where some parities align with smaller primes' contributions) can possibly have \(F(m) = k\) for small fixed \(k\).

Explicit computation for small \(m\) confirms that \(F(m)\) exists and equals 3 for some composite non-squares (e.g., \(m=6,8,10\)), but fails to equal 3 for many others (e.g., \(m=12,14,15\)), where larger \(k\) or non-existence may occur. For prime \(m\), the coordinate for \(p=m\) is \(1\) in \(v(m)\) and \(0\) in all prior vectors, so \(v(m)\) lies outside the span and \(F(m)\) does not exist. For prime powers \(p^e\) (\(e \geq 2\)), the parity of the \(p\)-exponent in \(m!\) is \((p^e - s_p(p^e))/(p-1)\) (with \(s_p\) the sum of base-\(p\) digits); this can be odd while all prior parities are even (e.g., for \(e=3\), \(p\) odd), potentially placing \(v(m)\) outside the span for some such \(m\).

Modeling the vectors as random yields that the expected number of \(m \leq n\) with \(F(m) = k\) (fixed \(k\)) is \(o(1)\), since \(\binom{m}{k-1} \ll 2^{\pi(m)}\) for large \(m\). However, the strong dependencies among the \(v(\ell)\) (each \(v(\ell)\) changes only at multiples of prime powers, and large-\(p\) coordinates are strictly nested) invalidate randomness. The constraints imply that \(m \in D_k\) only for \(m\) with highly atypical prime gaps near \(m\), whose density is governed by prime gap statistics. The number of such \(m \leq n\) is therefore expected to be \(o(n)\), but determining the precise order (e.g., whether \(\asymp n^{c}\) for some \(c<1\), or \(\asymp n/(\log n)^C\), or \(\gg n\) meaning \(\Omega(n)\)) requires a detailed analysis of how many \(m\) admit a selection of exactly \(k-1\) indices satisfying all nested parity conditions *and* the conditions for smaller primes, while no smaller selection works. This appears intractable with current methods, as it intertwines linear dependence over \(\mathbb{GF}(2)\) with the distribution of primes in short intervals.

For \(k=3\), the examples suggest \(|D_3 \cap [1,n]|\) grows slower than linearly (consistent with special configurations like \(m = p+1\) for primes \(p\) where a small \(a!\) makes \(a! \cdot m\) or \(a! \cdot \prod_{p+1}^m j\) square). As \(k\) increases to 6, more selections are available, allowing satisfaction of more parity conditions and thus potentially more \(m\), but still only those with prime gaps permitting a solution with *exactly* \(k-1\) terms (and no fewer). Whether this reaches \(\Omega(n)\) (positive density) for \(k=6\) remains open, as the typical number of large primes \(\sim m/(2\log m)\) grows with \(m\), outpacing any fixed \(k\).
