The given conditions imply that the family \((A_i)\) is almost disjoint in the sense that \(|A_i \cap A_j|\) is finite for all \(i \neq j\), but with the additional restriction that no two distinct members intersect in exactly one element.

First consider the intersection graph \(G\) on vertex set \(\{A_i\}\), with an edge between distinct \(A_i\) and \(A_j\) precisely when \(A_i \cap A_j \neq \emptyset\) (which forces \(|A_i \cap A_j| \geq 2\) by the given conditions). The connected components of \(G\) partition the index set. For distinct components \(C_1\) and \(C_2\), the corresponding unions \(U_{C_1} = \bigcup_{A \in C_1} A\) and \(U_{C_2} = \bigcup_{A \in C_2} A\) are disjoint. To see this, suppose there exists \(x \in U_{C_1} \cap U_{C_2}\). Then \(x \in A\) for some \(A \in C_1\) and \(x \in B\) for some \(B \in C_2\), so \(|A \cap B| \geq 1\). The assumption \(|A \cap B| \neq 1\) then forces \(|A \cap B| \geq 2\), whence \(A\) and \(B\) are adjacent in \(G\), contradicting that \(C_1\) and \(C_2\) are distinct components. Thus, it suffices to 2-color each \(U_C\) (for a fixed connected component \(C\)) so that every \(A \in C\) receives both colors; the colorings on distinct \(U_C\) can then be combined.

Now restrict attention to a fixed connected component \(C\), and let \(\mathcal{F} = \{A : A \in C\}\) with union \(U = \bigcup \mathcal{F}\). If \(|C| \leq \aleph_0\), then \(U\) is a countable union of countable sets, hence countable. Enumerate \(\mathcal{F} = \{A_n : n < \omega\}\) and initialize all points of \(U\) as uncolored. Proceed inductively on \(n\). At stage \(n\), at most finitely many sets \(A_0, \dots, A_{n-1}\) have been processed. Each such set intersects \(A_n\) in at most finitely many points (by almost disjointness) and causes at most two points of \(A_n\) to be colored (at most one point per missing color in that earlier set). Thus, only finitely many points of the infinite set \(A_n\) are colored so far. If both colors are already present in \(A_n\), do nothing. If only red appears, select an uncolored \(x \in A_n\) and color it blue. If only blue appears, select an uncolored \(x \in A_n\) and color it red. If no points are colored yet, select two distinct uncolored points in \(A_n\) and color one red and one blue. In all cases, both colors are now present in \(A_n\). After all stages, color any remaining uncolored points of \(U\) red. Each \(A_n\) receives both colors (and later colorings on points of \(A_n\), if any, cannot eliminate a color already present).

If \(|C| > \aleph_0\), the situation is more subtle, as a transfinite recursion over an enumeration \(\{A_\alpha : \alpha < \mu\}\) (\(\mu = |C|\)) may color all of a late \(A_\alpha\) before stage \(\alpha\), potentially in only one color (at which point the construction is stuck, as no uncolored point remains in \(A_\alpha\) to supply the missing color). While the countability of each individual \(A_\alpha\) implies that only countably many prior stages can have colored points inside it (as each coloring step affects at most two points), it remains possible in principle for all points of \(A_\alpha\) to be exhausted monochromatically by prior steps. Choosing points carefully at each stage \(\beta < \alpha\) (e.g., preferring points private to \(A_\beta\) among previously processed sets, which is always possible since only finitely many points of \(A_\beta\) lie in prior intersections) mitigates but does not obviously eliminate the risk for all uncountable components simultaneously.

Special cases of uncountable \(C\) are tractable. For instance, if all members of \(\mathcal{F}\) contain a common 2-element set \(\{x, y\}\) (so \(C\) forms a clique in \(G\)), color \(x\) red and \(y\) blue. This splits every member of \(\mathcal{F}\) at once (each contains both \(x\) and \(y\)), and the remaining points of \(U\) (typically consisting of "private" tails almost disjoint from each other) may be colored arbitrarily. More generally, if there is any finite \(F \subseteq U\) with \(|F| \geq 2\) that intersects every member of \(\mathcal{F}\) in at least one point, the elements of \(F\) can be 2-colored to split all members simultaneously if the coloring of \(F\) supplies both colors to each \(A \in \mathcal{F}\); but no such \(F\) need exist (e.g., in an infinite chain from \(G\), where consecutive members intersect in distinct pairs).

In the absence of the assumption that \(|A_i \cap A_j| \neq 1\), the claim is false: the "near-pencil" consisting of a single common point \(p\) together with continuum-many pairwise disjoint infinite "rays" (so all pairwise intersections have size exactly 1) yields a counterexample on a countable ground set. Coloring \(p\) red (without loss of generality) splits all sets via red, but splitting via blue requires the blue points to hit every ray; by disjointness of the rays this needs continuum-many blue points, which is impossible. The assumption \(|A_i \cap A_j| \neq 1\) rules out this and similar configurations (in which a single point "links" too many otherwise disjoint sets, forcing its color to propagate without sufficient freedom to split all sets).

The construction for countable components succeeds in ZFC, as does the construction for uncountable components in which a small set of shared points hits everything. The general case appears resistant to both direct construction (via recursion or choice of splitting sets) and to obvious counterexamples (which are blocked by the \(\neq 1\) assumption).
