# Grok Response for Problem 825

The problem asks whether there exists a fixed \(C > 0\) such that if \(\sigma(n) > Cn\), then there is a subset \(S\) of the proper divisors of \(n\) (i.e., the divisors strictly less than \(n\)) with \(\sum_{d \in S} d = n\).

Let \(s(n) = \sigma(n) - n\) denote the sum of the proper divisors of \(n\). The hypothesis \(\sigma(n) > Cn\) is then equivalent to \(s(n) > (C-1)n\). The desired conclusion is that \(n\) lies in the set of subset sums generated by the proper divisors. Equivalently (by taking complements), this holds if and only if \(s(n) - n = \sigma(n) - 2n\) lies in the set of subset sums generated by the proper divisors. Note that \(\sigma(n) - 2n > 0\) forces \(C > 2\) in any case where the implication could hold for all such \(n\).

To determine whether a uniform \(C\) exists, first consider the necessary density of divisors for large \(\sigma(n)/n\). It is well-known that \(\sigma(n)/n\) is maximized (to first order) when \(n\) is highly composite, in particular when \(n\) is a product of the smallest primes raised to nonincreasing powers. In such cases, the proper divisors necessarily include all integers up to some point (depending on the least common multiple of the initial integers), as well as multiples by the prime power factors. More precisely, if \(n\) is divisible by all primes up to \(y\), then every integer up to \(y\) is a proper divisor (for \(n > y\)).

Sort the proper divisors in increasing order: \(1 = d_1 < d_2 < \cdots < d_t \leq n/2\), where \(t = \tau(n) - 1\) and \(\tau(n)\) is the number of divisors of \(n\). A sufficient (but far from necessary) condition for the subset sums to include every integer from 0 to \(s(n)\) is that
\[
d_{i+1} \leq 1 + \sum_{j=1}^i d_j
\]
for each \(i = 1, \dots, t-1\). If this holds and \(s(n) > n\) (i.e., \(C > 2\)), then \(n\) is necessarily a subset sum. For highly abundant \(n\), the initial divisors are dense (including all small integers up to a large \(y\)), so the condition holds for small \(d_i\). However, gaps appear near the upper end: the largest proper divisor is at most \(n/2\), but the divisors near \(n/p_{\min}\) (where \(p_{\min}\) is the smallest prime dividing \(n\)) create potential violations of the inequality if the sum of all smaller divisors is too small relative to the gap size.

This sufficient condition thus fails to hold uniformly for all \(n\) with large \(\sigma(n)/n\), as one can always choose \(n\) with a sufficiently large prime power factor to force a gap exceeding the sum of all smaller divisors. This does not disprove the existence of \(C\), however, since the sufficient condition is strict and we only require hitting the single target value \(n\) (or equivalently \(\sigma(n) - 2n\)), not filling all integers up to \(s(n)\).

To hit \(n\) specifically, note that any proper divisor is at most \(n/2\), so at least two terms are required, and in practice (for even \(n\)) one typically requires including \(n/2\) and then hitting \(n/2\) with a subset of the remaining proper divisors all strictly less than \(n/2\). This suggests a recursive structure, but the recursion depth is bounded by the number of distinct prime factors (each step at least halves the target). However, the branching at each step depends on the specific factorization, and no uniform bound on the "deficit" \(\sigma(n) - 2n\) allows control of the recursion for arbitrary factorizations.

Now suppose for a contradiction that no such \(C\) exists. Then for every \(K > 0\) there must exist \(n\) with \(\sigma(n) > K n\) such that no subset of proper divisors sums to \(n\). But constructing such \(n\) explicitly is obstructed by the density of small divisors: if \(n\) is divisible by all primes up to \(y\) with \(y\) large enough that the sum of divisors up to \(y\) exceeds any fixed multiple of the largest gap size below \(n/2\), then one can always greedily subtract the largest possible proper divisor below the remaining target and use the small divisors to fill exactly (since the small divisors include a initial segment long enough to generate all residues and all values up to their total sum, which exceeds the gap). This heuristic suggests that \(\sigma(n)/n\) cannot be arbitrarily large while avoiding a subset sum of \(n\), but converting the heuristic to a rigorous bound on the abundance requires uniform control on the prime factors of \(n\), which is not available from known estimates on \(\sigma(n)/n\) alone (such as the maximal order \(\exp(c \log n / \log \log n)\)).

Conversely, suppose such a \(C\) does exist. Then all \(n\) with \(\sigma(n) > Cn\) must be semiperfect in the sense above. But this is obstructed by the existence of abundant \(n\) (i.e., with \(\sigma(n) > 2n\)) that fail the subset-sum condition, such as those where \(\sigma(n) - 2n\) cannot be formed from distinct proper divisors despite being smaller than the sum of the smallest proper divisors. Extending such examples to arbitrarily large \(\sigma(n)/n\) requires showing that the obstructions (gaps or modular obstructions modulo a prime factor) persist, but no such construction succeeds uniformly: multiplying by an additional large prime increases \(\sigma(n)/n\) only by a factor \(1 + 1/p < 2\), while introducing a gap larger than \(\sigma(m) + 1\) (for the cofactor \(m\)) that potentially skips over the target \(n\), but the small divisors from the cofactor still permit adjustments that hit \(n\) in all explicit cases.

After considering the density of divisors, the sufficient condition for gap-free subset sums, the complementary formulation in terms of summing to \(\sigma(n) - 2n\), the recursive structure obtained by subtracting large proper divisors such as \(n/2\), and heuristics for both existence and nonexistence of a uniform \(C\), no conclusion can be reached.
