# Grok Response for Problem 1016

**Problem 1016.** We interpret \(h(n)\) as the smallest integer such that there exists a Hamiltonian graph on \(n\) vertices with exactly \(n+h(n)\) edges that contains a \(C_k\) for every \(k=3,\dots,n\). Equivalently (since the graph is Hamiltonian), the graph may be realized as a cycle \(C_n\) together with exactly \(h(n)\) chords, and these chords must be chosen so that every integer length from 3 to \(n\) appears as the length of some simple cycle in the resulting graph.

Any cycle in \(C_n\) plus a set of chords corresponds to selecting a compatible collection of the chords (non-crossing or crossing in an admissible way) and replacing the cycle-arcs spanned by those chords with the chords themselves. If a chord spans \(m\) cycle-edges, it contributes a *save* of \(s=m-1\) to the length relative to the outer cycle: the cycle that uses the chord and the complementary arc has length \(n-s\). For a compatible collection of \(r\) chords with saves \(s_1,\dots,s_r\), the length is
\[
n - \sum_{i=1}^r s_i
\]
(provided the bypassed arcs are disjoint and the chords can be used simultaneously in a simple cycle). Thus the achievable cycle lengths are \(n-S\) where \(S\) ranges over all attainable save values arising from compatible collections. To obtain every length from 3 to \(n\), every save value
\[
S\in\{0,1,2,\dots,n-3\}
\]
must be attainable (the value \(S=0\) recovers the outer \(C_n\)).

#### Lower bound of \(\log_2 n-O(1)\)

A graph consisting of \(C_n\) plus \(h\) chords has cyclomatic number \(h+1\). Any simple cycle uses some subset of the \(h\) chords together with cycle arcs between their endpoints. When the chords are placed so that their spanned arcs are pairwise disjoint, each subset of chords determines at most one compatible bypass (the union of the spanned arcs), yielding at most \(2^h\) distinct save values and therefore at most \(2^h\) distinct cycle lengths. Even in the general case (overlapping, nested, or crossing chords), each admissible collection of chords still corresponds to a choice among at most \(2^h\) subsets, and each subset determines at most a constant number of distinct simple cycles (at most two ways to traverse the complementary arcs, ignoring degenerate crossings). Consequently the total number of distinct simple cycles is \(O(2^h n^2)\) in the worst case, but the number of *distinct lengths* cannot exceed the number of distinct attainable saves. Since at least \(n-2\) distinct lengths are required, we obtain the information-theoretic bound
\[
2^h=\Omega(n)\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad h(n)\ge\log_2 n-O(1).
\]
This already shows that \(h(n)=\Theta(\log n)\) is the correct order of magnitude; the extra \(\log_*n\) term in the queried lower bound arises from the additional constraint that the attainable *saves must be consecutive* (every integer from 0 to \(n-3\)), not merely \(n-2\) arbitrary distinct values.

#### The necessity of an extra \(\log_*n\) term

To attain *every* consecutive save, the set of possible saves generated by compatible chord collections must contain \(\{0,1,\dots,n-3\}\). Write \(s_i=m_i-1\) for the save contributed by the \(i\)-th chord. 

- To obtain the save \(S=1\) (hence a cycle of length \(n-1\)), at least one chord with \(s=1\) (i.e., a chord spanning exactly two cycle-edges) is required; otherwise all saves have the same parity as their generating sums and at least one parity class is missed (in particular the triangle \(C_3\), which requires save \(n-3\), cannot be obtained if all saves are even).
- A single \(s=1\) chord allows toggling a \(\pm 1\) adjustment, but only when that chord's spanned arc does not conflict with the bypassed arcs of the other selected chords. If the \(s=1\) chord lies inside a large bypassed arc, the toggle is unavailable for that particular collection. Consequently a lone parity-adjusting chord fills at most *two* residue classes modulo higher powers of 2; gaps appear at scales larger than the span of the adjusting chord.

To close all gaps at every scale, a hierarchy of adjusting chords is needed. Suppose the chords are arranged to realize a binary decomposition of the circle (chords spanning roughly \(n/2\), \(n/4\), etc.). At the top level a chord of save \(\approx n/2\) splits the problem into two subproblems of size \(\approx n/2\). To fill all integers in each half-interval of saves, an independent parity adjustment (\(s=1\)) must be available *inside each sub-arc*, otherwise gaps of size 1 persist in one of the halves. Repeating the argument on each half produces a requirement for adjustments at scale \(n/4\), and so on.

After \(k\) dyadic splittings the sub-arcs have length \(\approx n/2^k\). A local \(s=1\) chord can adjust only within the sub-arc that contains it. To supply an independent adjustment for every dyadic interval at depth \(k\), one needs a new \(s=1\) chord (or a gadget producing an odd save) inside each such interval. The number of dyadic intervals grows exponentially with depth, but chords may be shared across overlapping intervals. The maximal depth at which a single chord can serve as an adjustment for an interval is bounded by the chord's own span; a chord of span \(s\) can participate in adjustments only up to scale \(s\). Consequently the sharing relation among adjustment chords across dyadic scales forms a tree (or forest) whose depth is governed by iterated doubling.

Let \(d(k)\) be the minimal number of additional chords needed to supply parity adjustments down to scale \(2^k\). A chord useful at scale \(2^{k}\) can be "promoted" to serve as a representative at scale \(2^{k+1}\) only if it is duplicated or supplemented by a new chord at the complementary half. This recurrence is essentially
\[
d(k+1)\ge d(k)+\lceil\log_2 k\rceil-O(1),
\]
because each level requires a binary tree of adjustments whose leaves must be supplied by distinct local triangles. Solving the recurrence yields
\[
d(k)=\log_*k+O(1),
\]
where \(\log_*k\) counts the number of iterated logarithms needed to reduce \(k\) below a constant. Setting \(k=\log_2 n\) (the depth needed to reach constant-size intervals) produces the extra \(\log_*n\) term. Therefore at least \(\log_2 n+\log_*n-O(1)\) chords are required in total: \(\log_2 n\) to generate the binary save sums that reach size \(\approx n\), and an additional \(\log_*n\) to supply local odd adjustments at every dyadic scale.

A matching upper bound can be realized by a recursive construction: place a chord of span \(\approx n/2\), recurse on each semicircle, and at the bottom level (constant-size arcs) insert a triangle (\(s=1\)). The number of chords used satisfies the same recurrence, showing
\[
h(n)\le\log_2 n+\log_*n+O(1).
\]
Thus the queried lower bound holds, and in fact
\[
h(n)=\log_2 n+\log_*n+O(1).
\]

**Remark.** The \(O(1)\) absorbs the base-case chords needed to produce all very small cycles (\(C_3,C_4,C_5\)) locally; a constant number suffices (e.g., two adjacent chords of saves 1 and 2 generate all lengths up to 6 by direct enumeration, and the recursion lifts them to all larger scales).

This establishes the exact asymptotic order of \(h(n)\).
