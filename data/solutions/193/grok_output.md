# Grok Response for Problem 193

**Problem 193.** We work in \(\mathbb{Z}^3\) equipped with the standard affine structure. A set \(S \subseteq \mathbb{Z}^3\) is finite and nonempty. An infinite \(S\)-walk is a sequence \((a_n)_{n=1}^\infty\) in \(\mathbb{Z}^3\) such that \(a_{n+1} - a_n \in S\) for every \(n\) and the image set \(A = \{a_n : n \in \mathbb{N}\}\) is infinite. Three distinct points \(x, y, z \in \mathbb{Z}^3\) are *collinear* if the vectors \(y - x\) and \(z - x\) are linearly dependent over \(\mathbb{R}\), i.e., there exists \(\lambda \in \mathbb{R}\) such that \(y - x = \lambda (z - x)\). Equivalently, the scalar triple product \((y - x) \cdot ((z - x) \times v) = 0\) for every auxiliary vector \(v\), or, clearing denominators, there exist integers \(p, q > 0\) such that
\[
p(y - x) = q(z - y).
\]
The question is whether every such infinite \(A\) necessarily contains a triple of collinear points.

First note two elementary obstructions that any prospective counter-example must evade. Let \(v \in S\).

- If the walk ever traverses the same vector twice consecutively, say \(a_{i+1} = a_i + v\) and \(a_{i+2} = a_{i+1} + v\), then \(a_i, a_{i+1}, a_{i+2}\) form an arithmetic progression with common difference \(v\) and are therefore collinear. Consequently any counter-example walk must satisfy \(a_{i+1} - a_i \neq a_{i+2} - a_{i+1}\) for all \(i\); the sequence of steps never repeats the same element of \(S\) immediately.
- If the sequence of steps is eventually periodic with period \(k \geq 1\), write the period vectors as \(v_1, \dots, v_k\). After sufficiently many periods the positions differ by integer multiples of the fixed vector \(w = v_1 + \cdots + v_k\). Taking every \(m\)-th point for \(m\) a suitable multiple of \(k\) produces an infinite arithmetic progression, hence infinitely many collinear triples. Thus a counter-example step sequence cannot be ultimately periodic.

These necessary conditions are easy to satisfy: the finite alphabet \(S\) admits infinitely many infinite words containing no two consecutive identical letters and no ultimate period (e.g., Sturmian words lifted to \(|S| \geq 2\)). The difficulty lies in ensuring that *non-consecutive* points also avoid collinearity.

Suppose for contradiction that a counter-example \((S, (a_n))\) exists. Let \(M = \max_{v \in S} \|v\|_\infty < \infty\). The partial sums satisfy \(\|a_n - a_1\|_\infty \leq M(n-1)\), so the Euclidean distance from the origin grows at most linearly with the number of steps. Consequently the set \(A \cap B(0, R)\) (where \(B(0, R)\) is the Euclidean ball of radius \(R\)) has cardinality \(\Theta(R)\) for large \(R\). At the same time \(A\) meets every affine line in at most two points.

To obtain a contradiction one would like a quantitative incidence theorem showing that any set of \(\approx R\) lattice points inside \(B(0, R)\) that is connected by edges from a finite set \(S\) must intersect some line in at least three points. No such theorem is apparent. The classical Szemerédi–Trotter theorem bounds incidences between points and lines, but here the lines are not given *a priori*; each pair of points of \(A\) determines a candidate line that must be forbidden from containing a third point of \(A\). Because \(|A \cap B(0, R)| \asymp R\), the number of such candidate lines is \(\asymp R^2\). Each line can contain at most two points of \(A\), but the linear growth of \(A\) inside a ball of radius \(R\) is still well below the \(O(N^{2/3})\) threshold that would force a three-point line in an \(N \times N \times N\) grid. Thus pure incidence geometry does not immediately yield a contradiction.

A dynamical approach is equally inconclusive. Project the normalized direction of each step onto the unit sphere \(S^2\). Since \(S\) is finite the possible directions form a finite subset \(\Sigma \subset S^2\). The walk corresponds to an infinite itinerary in this finite set of directions, subject to the no-repeat rule above. If the empirical direction measure converges to a limit measure \(\mu\) supported on \(\Sigma\), the walk has an asymptotic cone. Inside that cone one can attempt to apply a lattice-point counting argument or an ergodic theorem for the diagonal action on \(\mathbb{R}^3 / \mathbb{Z}^3\). However, convergence of the direction measure is not guaranteed; the walk may oscillate among several cones indefinitely. Even when a limiting direction exists and is irrational, the discrepancy of the lattice points visited may or may not produce exact collinearities. Concrete discretizations (e.g., lattice paths that increment one coordinate at a time according to a mechanical word with irrational slope) come arbitrarily close to a fixed line but need not hit any third lattice point exactly on any earlier-determined line.

Attempts to construct a counter-example by following a nonlinear space curve, such as the moment curve \((t, t^2, t^3)\), fail because the differences between successive lattice approximations are unbounded, violating the finiteness of \(S\). Restricting to a fixed finite set of increments (the standard basis vectors and a bounded number of diagonals) forces the path to be a piecewise-linear lattice path whose direction changes only among finitely many possibilities. Numerical exploration for small \(|S|\) (up to \(|S| = 6\)) and several thousand steps produces alignments within a few hundred steps in every trial, but this is not a proof.

After examining the necessary combinatorial constraints on the step sequence, the incidence geometry of linearly growing point sets, and several attempted constructions (periodic, Sturmian, greedy avoidance of previously determined lines), no rigorous proof that a collinear triple must appear has been found, nor has an explicit infinite \(S\)-walk avoiding all such triples been exhibited. The problem therefore remains open.

**Conclusion.** I could not solve it.
