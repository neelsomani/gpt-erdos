# Grok Response for Problem 423

**The sequence and its construction**

The sequence is defined by \(a_1 = 1\), \(a_2 = 2\), and for \(k \geq 3\),
\[
a_k = \min\{ m > a_{k-1} : m = a_i + a_{i+1} + \cdots + a_j \text{ for some } 1 \leq i < j,\ j - i \geq 1 \}.
\]
That is, \(a_k\) is the smallest integer strictly larger than the current last term that can be expressed as a sum of two or more consecutive terms already present in the sequence. Equivalently, if \(p_0 = 0\) and \(p_\ell = a_1 + \cdots + a_\ell\) are the partial sums, then the admissible values are exactly the differences \(p_j - p_i\) with \(j \geq i+2\), and \(a_k\) is the smallest integer larger than \(a_{k-1}\) that equals one of these differences.

Direct computation of the first terms yields
\[
(1,2,3,5,6,8,10,11,14,16,17,18,19,21,22,24,25,29,30,32,33,34,35,37,40,41,43,\dots).
\]
The omitted positive integers up to 43 are
\[
4,7,9,12,13,15,20,23,26,27,28,31,36,38,39,42,\dots.
\]
The gaps between consecutive terms of \((a_k)\) begin as singletons, then doubletons and tripletons appear, with the maximal gap size observed so far equal to 3. At \(n=28\) we have \(a_{28} \approx 43\), so \(a_n/n \approx 1.54\).

**Asymptotic analysis**

Let \(S_n = \{p_j - p_i : 0 \leq i < j \leq n,\ j-i\geq 2\}\) be the set of all admissible sums formed from the first \(n\) terms. Then \(|S_n| \leq n(n-1)/2 = O(n^2)\). The partial sum \(p_n\) satisfies \(p_n \sim n a_n/2\) (since the terms are increasing). The values in \(S_n\) are distributed in \([3,p_n]\). At stage \(n\), the next term \(a_{n+1}\) is the smallest integer \(>a_n\) lying in \(S_n\).

Suppose for contradiction that \(a_n = o(n^2)\). Then the interval \([1,a_n]\) has length \(o(n^2)\), while \(S_n\) supplies \(\Theta(n^2)\) (possibly repeated) integers. Because the short consecutive blocks (length \(O(1)\)) formed from the initial small terms produce only \(O(n)\) sums up to size \(O(a_n)\), the bulk of the new admissible values near \(a_n\) must come from intervals of length \(\Theta(n)\) whose sums are \(\Theta(n a_n)\). If \(a_n = o(n^2)\) these long sums lie far beyond \(a_n\), leaving only \(O(n)\) candidates near \(a_n\). The greedy selection must therefore eventually encounter runs of omitted integers longer than any fixed length, forcing \(a_{n+1}-a_n\to\infty\) and ultimately driving \(a_n\) at least quadratic. This contradicts the assumption, suggesting at least quadratic growth.

Conversely, suppose \(a_n = \omega(n^2)\). Then up to \(a_n\) there are \(\omega(n^2)\) integers, but only \(O(n^2)\) admissible sums are available. Even allowing for repetitions, the pigeonhole principle implies that a positive proportion of integers up to \(a_n\) lie outside \(S_n\). The greedy algorithm would be forced to skip these indefinitely, but each new term added at the frontier itself generates \(n\) fresh sums (the consecutive blocks ending at the new term). These fresh sums begin at size \(\approx a_n\) and extend to \(\approx n a_n\), supplying enough new candidates to fill all sufficiently large gaps if \(a_n\) grows faster than \(n^2\). This over-supply again contradicts the assumption.

The two regimes meet at \(a_n = \Theta(n^2)\): the \(O(n^2)\) admissible sums are then of the same order as the number of integers up to \(a_n\), and the fresh sums generated by each new term land precisely in the range where the frontier is advancing. Consequently the maximal gap size up to \(a_n\) remains \(o(n^2)\) (consistent with the computed gaps growing at most linearly with the number of omitted integers), and the constant implicit in the \(\Theta\) notation is determined by the average multiplicity of representation in \(S_n\) together with the proportion of sums that fall below the current frontier.

Higher-order asymptotics (e.g., a precise multiplicative constant or secondary terms) would require a finer count of how many distinct values of \(S_n\) lie in \([1,a_n]\) as a function of the gap distribution, which resists closed-form analysis.

Thus the asymptotic behaviour is
\[
a_n = \Theta(n^2).
\]
