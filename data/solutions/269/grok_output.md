**The Sequence and LCM**

Let \(P = \{p_1, \dots, p_k\}\) with \(k \geq 2\) and \(p_1 < \cdots < p_k\). The set underlying the sequence \(\{a_n\}\) is the multiplicative semigroup \(S\) generated by \(P\) (including \(1\)). Thus each \(a \in S\) is of the form \(\prod_{i=1}^k p_i^{e_i}\) with \(e_i \geq 0\), and \(\{a_n\}\) enumerates \(S\) in increasing order.

For any \(m \in S\), let
\[
L(m) := \prod_{p \in P} p^{\lfloor \log_p m \rfloor}.
\]
The exponent \(\lfloor \log_p m \rfloor\) equals the largest \(e\) such that \(p^e \leq m\). Because \(p^e\) is the smallest element of \(S\) divisible by \(p^e\), the value of \(L(a_n)\) increases precisely when \(a_n = p^e\) for some \(p \in P\) and \(e \geq 1\). In particular, \(L_n := [a_1, \dots, a_n] = L(a_n)\), the partial sums are
\[
s_n = \sum_{m=1}^n \frac{1}{L_m} = \frac{b_n}{L_n}
\]
for an integer \(b_n \geq 1\), and \(L_k \mid L_n\) whenever \(k \leq n\).

The infinite sum is
\[
S = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{L_n} = \sum_{m \in S} \frac{1}{L(m)}.
\]
Each term is positive and \(L_n \to \infty\) (new prime powers appear infinitely often), so \(0 < S - s_n \to 0\).

**Grouping by Constant LCM**

Fix exponents \(A, B \geq 0\) (the argument extends to \(|P| = k \geq 2\) by replacing pairs with \(k\)-tuples). Let
\[
I(A, B) = \bigl[ \max(2^A, 3^B),\ \min(2^{A+1}, 3^{B+1}) \bigr)
\]
(using \(P = \{2, 3\}\) for explicitness; the general case is analogous). Define \(N(A, B)\) to be the number of elements of \(S\) lying in \(I(A, B)\). The interval is nonempty only for those \((A, B)\) satisfying
\[
\max(2^A, 3^B) < \min(2^{A+1}, 3^{B+1}),
\]
i.e., \(|A \log 2 - B \log 3| < O(1)\). The sum then reads
\[
S = \sum_{A=0}^\infty \sum_{B=0}^\infty \frac{N(A, B)}{2^A \cdot 3^B},
\]
where the outer sum runs only over pairs for which \(I(A, B) \neq \emptyset\).

Because \(\log 3 / \log 2\) is irrational, the fractional parts \(\{B \log 3 / \log 2\}\) are dense in \([0, 1)\). Consequently the relative sizes of \(2^A\) and \(3^B\) fluctuate, and the length ratio of each nonempty \(I(A, B)\) is bounded by \(\max(2, 3) = 3\). Thus each time \(L_n\) increases it is multiplied by either \(2\) or \(3\).

For each fixed \((A, B)\) the count \(N(A, B)\) equals the number of lattice points \((x, y) \in \mathbb{N}_0^2\) whose weighted sum \(x \log 2 + y \log 3\) falls inside an interval of logarithmic length \(O(1)\). When \(A \approx c B\) with \(c = \log 3 / \log 2\), there are \(\Theta(A + B)\) candidate \(y\)-values up to \(\approx A \log 2 / \log 3\), and for each \(y\) usually \(O(1)\) compatible \(x\). Hence \(N(A, B) = O(A + B) = O(\log L)\) whenever \(L = 2^A 3^B\).

**Tail Estimates**

After any \(n\), the tail satisfies
\[
t_n := S - s_n = \sum_{m > n} \frac{1}{L_m}.
\]
While \(L_m\) remains constant the contribution is exactly \(N/L_n\) for the pertinent \(N = N(A, B)\). Because each successive denominator is obtained by multiplying the previous by \(2\) or \(3\), the tail after a segment with denominator \(L\) is bounded above by a sum of the form
\[
t_n \leq \sum_{j=0}^\infty O\bigl((\log (C^j L)) / (C^j L)\bigr)
\]
where \(C \in \{2, 3\}\) according to which prime's exponent increases. The series is \(O((\log L)/L)\). A matching lower bound holds because at least the next term is \(\geq 1/(3L)\). Thus
\[
c_1 \frac{\log L_n}{L_n} \leq t_n \leq c_2 \frac{\log L_n}{L_n}
\]
for absolute constants \(c_1, c_2 > 0\) (the constants fluctuate mildly with the Diophantine properties of \(\log 3 / \log 2\), but remain \(\Theta(1)\)).

**Attempted Proof that \(S\) Is Irrational**

Suppose for contradiction that \(S = p/q \in \mathbb{Q}\) in lowest terms. For the partial sum \(s_n = b_n / L_n\) we have
\[
\biggl| \frac{p}{q} - \frac{b_n}{L_n} \biggr| = t_n \geq \frac{1}{q L_n},
\]
because the left-hand side is a nonzero rational with denominator dividing \(q L_n\). If there existed infinitely many \(n\) with \(t_n < 1/(q L_n)\), this would be impossible.

The bound \(t_n = O((\log L_n)/L_n)\) is only larger than \(1/(q L_n)\) by a logarithmic factor, so it does not yield the desired contradiction. When \(N(A, B) = 1\) (which occurs infinitely often, e.g., near good approximations such as \(3^5 = 243 < 256 = 2^8\)) the immediate contribution is exactly \(1/L\), but the subsequent segment begins at a denominator at most \(3L\). The tail therefore remains \(\Theta((\log L)/L)\) rather than \(o(1/L)\). Because the interval length ratio is uniformly bounded, jumps in \(L_n\) never exceed a constant factor; the logarithmic multiplicity \(N(A, B) = O(\log L)\) cannot be avoided often enough to force \(t_n = o(1/L_n)\).

**p-adic Valuations**

Let \(v_2\) be the \(2\)-adic valuation. Then \(v_2(s_n) = v_2(b_n) - v_2(L_n)\). The numerator \(b_n\) accumulates earlier terms whose denominators divide \(L_n\), but cancellations can occur. For the geometric series \(\sum 2^{-k}\) the partial sums have \(v_2(s_n) \to -\infty\) while the limit \(2\) has \(v_2(2) = 1 > -\infty\). The same phenomenon appears here: \(v_2(t_n)\) tends to be large and negative precisely when many doublings of the power of \(2\) remain in the tail, yet the archimedean size of \(t_n\) is still \(\Theta((\log L_n)/L_n)\). Thus unbounded negative valuations of the approximants do not preclude convergence to a rational.

**Conclusion**

The Diophantine properties of \(\log p_i / \log p_j\) produce segments with \(N(A, B) = 1\) infinitely often, yet the uniformly bounded jumps in successive values of \(L_n\) keep every tail comparable to \((\log L_n)/L_n\). Consequently the approximations \(s_n\) are never sufficiently sharp to contradict the assumption that \(S\) is rational, nor do valuation arguments yield a contradiction.
