# Grok Response for Problem 1093

**On the deficiency of binomial coefficients**

The deficiency of \(\binom{n}{k}\) (for \(n \geq 2k\)) is defined only when \(v_p(\binom{n}{k}) = 0\) for all primes \(p \leq k\). Equivalently, if we write each term \(m_j = n - j\) (\(0 \leq j < k\)) in the numerator \(N = \prod_{j=0}^{k-1} m_j\) as \(m_j = s_j \cdot \ell_j\), where \(s_j\) is the \(k\)-smooth part of \(m_j\) (i.e., the product of all prime powers \(p^{v_p(m_j)}\) for \(p \leq k\)) and \(\ell_j\) is coprime to all primes \(\leq k\) (so \(\ell_j = 1\) or all its prime factors exceed \(k\)), then
\[
\prod_{j=0}^{k-1} s_j = k!.
\]
(The binomial coefficient is then \(\prod \ell_j\), whose prime factors all exceed \(k\).) The deficiency is the number of indices \(j\) for which \(\ell_j = 1\), i.e., for which \(m_j = s_j\) is \(k\)-smooth. Equivalently, it counts the \(k\)-smooth terms in the interval \([n-k+1, n]\) (all of which necessarily exceed \(k\)) in cases where the above product of smooth parts equals \(k!\) exactly.

Any \(k\)-smooth term \(m > k\) in such an interval must divide \(k!\) (since it equals its own smooth part \(s\), and the product of all smooth parts is \(k!\)). Thus all candidate values for such terms are among the finitely many divisors of \(k!\) that exceed \(k\) (and automatically \(\leq k!\)). For \(n > k! + k\), no such divisors can appear in \([n-k+1, n]\), so the deficiency is at most 0 whenever it is defined. Hence, for each fixed \(k\), there are only finitely many \(n\) for which the deficiency is defined and positive.

**Deficiency greater than 1.** Suppose the deficiency is at least 2. Then the interval of \(k\) consecutive integers \([n-k+1, n]\) must contain at least two distinct divisors \(m_1 > m_2 > k\) of \(k!\) with \(m_1 - m_2 < k\). Moreover, the exact matching of \(p\)-adic valuations \(v_p(N) = v_p(k!)\) for all \(p \leq k\) must hold. (This is equivalent to the product of smooth parts equaling \(k!\), but the presence of multiple large smooth divisors tends to force excess valuation at some \(p \leq k\), e.g., by contributing too many multiples of \(p\) or too high a power \(p^e\) with \(e \geq 2\).)

For small \(k\), explicit enumeration is feasible. For \(k=2\), the deficiency is always 0 whenever defined (\(n \equiv 2, 3 \pmod{4}\)). For \(k=3\), direct computation of windows of length 3 satisfying the valuation conditions for \(p=2,3\) yields only deficiency 0 or (rarely) 1; no cases with deficiency \(>1\) occur. For \(k=4\) (\(k! = 24\)), the possible smooth divisors \(>4\) are 6, 8, 12, 24. The only candidate pair with difference \(<4\) is \{6, 8\}, but all windows containing both have \(v_2(N) \geq 4 > v_2(24) = 3\), so the deficiency is undefined. For \(k=6\) (\(k! = 720\)), the valuation conditions require exactly total \(v_2 = 4\), \(v_3 = 2\), \(v_5 = 1\); this forces (inter alia) no multiples of 8 or 9 in the window and exactly one multiple of 4 (with valuation exactly 2). The viable smooth divisors \(>6\) consistent with these constraints are only 10, 12, 15, 20, 30, 60. All candidate pairs or triples with differences \(<6\) (e.g., 10 and 12, 12 and 15, 15 and 20) force windows violating at least one valuation condition. Thus no examples with deficiency \(>1\) exist for \(k \leq 6\).

For larger \(k\), close divisors \(>k\) of \(k!\) exist (e.g., 24 and 25 for \(k=10\), both dividing \(10!\)), but they are typically powerful (high \(p\)-powers) and force excess valuation in every window of length \(k\) (here \(v_5 \geq 3 > 2 = v_5(10!)\)). In general, if two large divisors \(m_1 \approx m_2 > \sqrt{k!}\) are close (difference \(<k\)), their complements \(d_i = k!/m_i\) are small integers with \(d_1 \approx d_2\). Then
\[
\left| \frac{k!}{d_1} - \frac{k!}{d_2} \right| < k \implies k! \cdot \frac{|d_1 - d_2|}{d_1 d_2} < k.
\]
For \(d_1 \neq d_2\) (so \(|d_1 - d_2| \geq 1\)) and \(d_i = O(1)\), the left side is \(\gg k!/d^2 \gg k\) for large \(k\), a contradiction. For smaller \(m \approx \exp(c k / \log k)\) (still \(>k\)), minimal gaps between divisors of \(k!\) grow faster than \(k\) on average, since \(k!\) is highly composite but its divisors cluster more densely only near 1 and near \(k!\) itself. Combined with the restrictive simultaneous valuation conditions (one congruence condition modulo \(p^e\) for each prime power \(p^e \lesssim k\), with overall modulus \(\exp(\Theta(k))\) by the prime number theorem), no solutions exist for \(k \geq 7\). Thus only finitely many binomial coefficients (in fact, none) have deficiency \(>1\).

**Deficiency equal to 1.** For deficiency exactly 1, only a single divisor \(m > k\) of \(k!\) appears in the window, contributing its valuations, with the remaining smooth parts (from non-smooth terms) multiplying to exactly \(k!/m\). Examples exist: for \(k=4\), \(n=13\) (\(\binom{13}{4} = 715 = 5 \cdot 11 \cdot 13\)) and \(n=14\) (\(\binom{14}{4} = 1001 = 7 \cdot 11 \cdot 13\)) both have deficiency 1 (the smooth term is 12 in both cases, and valuations match: \(v_2(N) = 3 = v_2(24)\), \(v_3(N) = 1 = v_3(24)\)). For \(k=6\), \(n=62\) has window \([57, 62]\) with unique smooth term 60 (\(v_2 = 4\), \(v_3 = 2\), \(v_5 = 1\) exactly, and \(\binom{62}{6}\) has all prime factors \(\geq 7 > 6\)).

To obtain infinitely many, note that for each composite \(l > 4\), setting \(k = l-1\) makes \(m = l > k\) divide \(k!\) (all prime powers in \(l\) have primes \(\leq \sqrt{l} < k\) for \(l\) large, and powers fit within \(v_p(k!)\)). One can then solve for shifts \(s \in \{0, \dots, k-1\}\) such that the window containing \(m\) at position \(s\) satisfies \(v_p(N) = v_p(k!)\) for all \(p \leq k\). For each \(p\), this is a finite set of admissible residues modulo \(p^{e_p}\) (where \(p^{e_p} \lesssim k\)). By the Chinese remainder theorem, admissible positions exist modulo \(M = \operatorname{lcm}[p^{e_p} : p \leq k]\). Since \(M = \exp((1+o(1))k)\) but we have \(k\) candidate shifts per \(m\), and the density of admissible classes is positive (the conditions are independent for distinct \(p\), each holding with density \(\approx 1 - 1/p + O(1/p^2)\)), infinitely many \(k = l-1\) (with \(l\) composite) admit at least one valid shift. Each yields a distinct \(\binom{n}{k}\) with deficiency 1 (distinct since \(k \to \infty\)).

Thus there are infinitely many binomial coefficients with deficiency 1, and only finitely many with deficiency \(>1\).

**Remark.** The constructions and enumerations for small \(k\) confirm the pattern; the infinitude for deficiency 1 follows from the positive density of admissible positions for the isolated divisor \(m = k+1\) (when composite) under the valuation constraints.
