# Grok Response for Problem 467

The statement requires the existence of residue classes \(a_p \pmod{p}\) (one for each prime \(p \leq x\)) and a partition of the primes \(\leq x\) into nonempty sets \(A\) and \(B\) such that the collection of arithmetic progressions \(\{n < x : n \equiv a_p \pmod{p}\}\) for \(p \in A\) covers \(\{0, 1, \dots, \lfloor x \rfloor - 1\}\), and likewise for \(B\).

To show existence, one approach is to partition the primes \(\leq x\) into "small" (those \(\leq y\), for a parameter \(y = y(x)\) to be chosen) and "large" (those in \((y, x]\)), then split the small primes between \(A\) and \(B\) so that each of \(A_\text{small}\) and \(B_\text{small}\) has \(\sum 1/p \approx \frac12 \log\log y\). For a fixed choice of the \(a_p\) with \(p \in A_\text{small}\), the set \(\text{Bad}_A\) of \(n < x\) not covered by any such progression has size \(\sim x \prod_{p \in A_\text{small}} (1 - 1/p) \asymp x / (\log y)^{1/2}\) (with the implied constant independent of the specific choice of residues, by the Chinese Remainder Theorem applied up to the primorial \(\ll e^y\)). The large primes assigned to \(A\) must then be used to cover \(\text{Bad}_A\) by appropriate choice of their \(a_p\).

Each prime \(p > y\) covers \(\lfloor x/p \rfloor\) or \(\lfloor x/p \rfloor + 1\) terms in \(\{0, \dots, \lfloor x \rfloor - 1\}\). If these can be targeted at elements of \(\text{Bad}_A\) (possible for \(p > n\) by setting \(a_p = n\)), then in principle the large primes in \(A\) have total covering capacity \(\sum_{y < p \leq x, p \in A} x/p \asymp x \cdot \frac12 \log\log(x/y)\). A similar computation applies to \(B\).

Balancing the parameters to ensure \(|\text{Bad}_A|\) is not much larger than the covering capacity of the large primes in \(A\) (and likewise for \(B\)), while also ensuring that the large primes can be assigned without conflict to both sides and that gaps between primes do not prevent targeting large \(n < x\), leads to incompatible requirements on \(y\). Taking \(y \asymp \log x\) makes \(|\text{Bad}_A| \asymp x / \sqrt{\log\log x}\) (too many exceptions for the \(\asymp x/\log x\) large primes to cover, since each such \(p\) covers \(O(1)\) terms). Taking \(y \asymp x^\theta\) for fixed \(\theta > 0\) makes the main term for \(|\text{Bad}_A|\) of order \(x/(\theta \log x)\), again too large relative to the covering capacity unless the residues for small primes in \(A\) are chosen to force atypically large cancellation in the count of \(\text{Bad}_A\) (beyond what is guaranteed by inclusion-exclusion or Siegel-Walfisz-type estimates on the error). Taking \(y\) extremely close to \(x\) leaves too few large primes on one side of the partition.

A probabilistic approach (choosing each \(a_p\) uniformly and independently, and independently coloring each prime red/blue with equal probability to define \(A\) and \(B\)) yields that for each fixed \(n < x\), the probability that \(n\) is uncovered by (say) all red primes is exactly \(\prod_{p \leq x} (1 - 1/(2p)) \asymp K / \sqrt{\log x}\) for an absolute constant \(K > 0\) (by Mertens' theorem). Union bound over the \(\asymp x\) choices of \(n\) and the two colors is then \(\gg 1\), so inconclusive. The Lovász local lemma is inapplicable in its standard form, as each bad event (a specific \(n\) uncovered by one color) depends on the colors and all \(a_p\) (\(p \leq x\)), yielding a dependency graph with maximum degree \(\asymp x\).

Direct constructions (e.g., all \(a_p = 0\), or all \(a_p = 1\), or splitting primes by size) fail for similar reasons: one side of the partition inevitably leaves \(\gg 1\) uncovered \(n < x\). Small \(x\) (e.g., \(x = 5\)) provides a sanity check that at least one side fails to cover when it receives too few primes.

After considering these approaches and variants (including second-moment methods on the number of uncovered \(n\), greedy assignment of large primes to exceptions, and weighted colorings), no proof is obtained.
