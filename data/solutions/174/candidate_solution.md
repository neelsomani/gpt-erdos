This is a main open problem in **Euclidean Ramsey theory**, so there is **no complete proved characterisation** known today.

What we *do* know is the following.

## What must be true

### Ramsey  (\Rightarrow)  spherical

If a finite set $A$ is Ramsey, then $A$ must lie on the surface of a (finite‑radius) sphere.
In other words, there must exist a point $c$ and (r>0) such that (|a-c|=r) for every (a\in A). 

So **non‑spherical** configurations are **not** Ramsey (for example, three equally spaced collinear points). 

## What is conjectured (and would be the “clean” characterisation)

### Spherical sets conjecture (Graham / Erdős–Graham–Montgomery–Rothschild–Spencer–Straus)

A finite set $A$ is Ramsey **if and only if** it is spherical.

The “only if” direction is the theorem above. The “if” direction is still open and is considered the central conjecture in the area. 

So, the best “characterisation” people expect is:

[
A \text{ is Ramsey } \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad A \text{ is spherical,}
]

but this is not proved in full.

## Big proved classes of Ramsey sets (partial positive results)

Even though the full conjecture is open, many spherical sets are known to be Ramsey:

### All simplices are Ramsey

Every non‑degenerate simplex (affinely independent set of points: any triangle, any tetrahedron, etc.) is Ramsey. 

### Products give “rectangular” Ramsey sets

If $X$ and $Y$ are Ramsey then (X\times Y) is Ramsey; in particular, “rectangular” vertex sets (rectangles/bricks/boxes in any dimension) are Ramsey. 

### Sets with enough symmetry (Kříž)

If a finite set $X$ has a **transitive solvable** group of isometries acting on it, then $X$ is Ramsey. This implies, for example, that the vertex sets of **regular polygons** are Ramsey (and many other highly symmetric configurations). 

(And of course any **subset** of a Ramsey set is Ramsey, since a monochromatic copy of the bigger set contains one of the subset.)

## A rival conjecture

Leader–Russell–Walters proposed a different possible description: Ramsey sets might be exactly the **subtransitive** ones (subsets of some finite transitive configuration). This is also open, and it is known that it is genuinely different from “spherical”, because there are spherical sets that are *not* subtransitive. 

## Bottom line

* **Proved:** $A$ Ramsey (\Rightarrow) $A$ spherical. 
* **Conjectured (the hoped‑for characterisation):** $A$ Ramsey (\Leftrightarrow) $A$ spherical. 
* **Proved large families:** simplices, rectangles/bricks (via products), regular polygons, and more generally many sets coming from transitive solvable symmetry.
