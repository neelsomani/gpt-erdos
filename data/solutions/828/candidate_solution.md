No one currently knows how to prove (or disprove) that this holds **for every** integer $a$.

What *is* known is that for many specific $a$ there are easy infinite families, but for some other $a$ the question already runs into difficult, long‑studied problems.

## Easy infinite families for some $a$

* **(a=-1)**: take $n=p$ prime. Then (\varphi(p)=p-1\mid p-1=n-1=n+a). So infinitely many $n$.

* **$a=0$**: take (n=2^k). Then (\varphi(2^k)=2^{k-1}\mid 2^k=n). So infinitely many $n$.

* **More generally, for many negative $a$**: if (a=-m) with (\varphi(m)\mid m), then for any prime (p\nmid m),
  [
  n=mp \quad\Rightarrow\quad \varphi(n)=\varphi(m),(p-1)\mid m(p-1)=mp-m=n+a,
  ]
  giving infinitely many solutions. [[nomath]](E.g. $a=-2$ via $n=2p$, $a=-4$ via $n=4p$, etc.)[[/nomath]]

So the statement is certainly true for lots of $a$.

## But the “for every $a$” claim runs into an open problem [[nomath]](already at $a=1$)[[/nomath]]

Take **$a=1$**. Then you are asking whether there are infinitely many $n$ such that
[
\varphi(n)\mid n+1.
]
The known solutions form OEIS sequence A203966, beginning
[
1,,2,,3,,15,,255,,65535,,83623935,,4294967295,,6992962672132095,\dots
]
([OEIS][1])

A key remark there is that any solution with ((n+1)/\varphi(n)=3) would have to be astronomically large [[nomath]](and similarly for $\ge 4$)[[/nomath]], and in fact **all solutions below (1.6\times 10^{30})** satisfy ((n+1)/\varphi(n)=2), i.e. solve
[
2\varphi(n)=n+1,
]
and hence coincide (in that range) with OEIS A050474. ([OEIS][1])

Now, the equation (2\varphi(n)=n+1) is itself a famous hard Diophantine problem studied since Lehmer (1932), and OEIS A050474 records that only **two “independent”** solutions are known (from which others are generated by a simple construction), and that **there are no other terms below (10^{25})**. ([OEIS][2])

All of this is strong evidence that the $a=1$ case is **nontrivial and not settled**, and (as reflected in the OEIS notes) it is not currently known whether there are infinitely many $n$ with (\varphi(n)\mid n+1). ([OEIS][1])

## Conclusion

* For many $a$ (e.g. (a=-1,0,-2,-4,\dots)) there are **obvious infinite families** of solutions.
* But the blanket statement “**for every** (a\in\mathbb Z), there are infinitely many $n$ with (\varphi(n)\mid n+a)” is **not known**, because even the special case $a=1$ is tied to difficult, currently unresolved questions. ([OEIS][1])
