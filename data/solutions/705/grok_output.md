# Grok Response for Problem 705

**Problem 705.** We consider finite unit distance graphs \(G\) in \(\mathbb{R}^2\): the vertex set is a finite subset \(V \subset \mathbb{R}^2\), and two vertices are adjacent precisely when their Euclidean distance is exactly \(1\).

The question is whether there exists a constant \(k \geq 3\) such that every such \(G\) with girth at least \(k\) (i.e., containing no cycle of length \(3,4,\dots,k-1\)) satisfies \(\chi(G) \leq 3\).

A positive answer would mean that, for all sufficiently large girth, the geometric constraint of realizing all edges by distance exactly \(1\) in the Euclidean plane forces the graph to be \(3\)-colorable. A negative answer would require, for every \(k\), the existence of a finite point set whose unit-distance graph has girth \(\geq k\) yet needs at least four colors.

**Local geometric constraints imposed by large girth.**  
Fix a vertex \(v \in V\). Its neighbors lie on the circle of radius \(1\) centered at \(v\). A cycle of length \(3\) through \(v\) arises precisely when two neighbors \(u,w\) satisfy \(\|u-w\|=1\), i.e., when the angle \(\angle uvw\) equals exactly \(\pi/3\). Thus girth \(>3\) forbids any pair of neighbors from subtending exactly \(\pi/3\) at \(v\), but permits angular separations arbitrarily close to \(\pi/3\) (or any other value except exactly \(\pi/3\)).

A cycle of length \(4\) can arise in several ways: two common neighbors at distance \(1\) from each other and from a pair of non-adjacent vertices, or a rhombus with side length \(1\) and a non-\(\pi/3\) angle whose diagonals avoid length \(1\), etc. Forbidding all such configurations imposes that certain algebraic relations among coordinates (polynomial equations of degree \(2\) coming from the law of cosines) cannot hold simultaneously. As girth grows, more and more of these low-degree algebraic conditions are forbidden.

**Global coloring consequences.**  
If the girth is large, the graph is locally tree-like: the ball of radius \(r < k/2\) about any vertex is a tree. In a pure graph-theoretic sense this does not cap the chromatic number (Erdős’ probabilistic construction yields graphs of arbitrarily large girth and chromatic number). Here, however, the embedding supplies extra structure: all edges are rigid length \(1\), so the possible ways to attach further vertices are constrained by the geometry of the plane. In particular, continuing a path while avoiding short cycles forces the directions of successive edges to avoid a dense set of forbidden angles (the angles that would close a short cycle). Because the forbidden angles are algebraic over \(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{3})\) (generated by iterated law-of-cosines relations with side \(1\)), for sufficiently large girth the allowed directions become “sparse” on the circle.

One might hope that this sparsity permits a \(3\)-coloring by a greedy argument that propagates colors consistently around large cycles. Suppose we attempt to \(3\)-color breadth-first from a root. At each step the color of a new vertex is forbidden only by its already-colored neighbors (at most two in a tree-like neighborhood). The geometric constraint ensures that the set of positions reachable in \(t\) steps without short cycles cannot contain three mutually distance-\(1\) vertices that would force a fourth color. Unfortunately, this intuition does not immediately yield a global obstruction: a large odd wheel or a Mycielski-type construction realized with unit distances could still force a fourth color, provided all its short cycles are eliminated. Constructing (or ruling out) such an object appears combinatorially and algebraically intricate; each additional vertex introduces a quadratic extension of the coordinate field, and forbidding short cycles eliminates many of these extensions.

**Attempts at constructions.**  
- A regular \(n\)-gon with side length exactly \(1\) (radius \(1/(2\sin(\pi/n))\)) yields exactly the cycle \(C_n\) once \(n\) is large enough that no chord of length \(2,3,\dots,\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1\) equals \(1\). For odd \(n \geq k\) this gives girth \(n\) and \(\chi=3\), consistent with the claimed bound but not a counter-example.  
- Superimposing several such large odd cycles that intersect in controlled ways can produce girth-\(>4\) unit-distance graphs that are not bipartite, yet all examined examples remain \(3\)-colorable.  
- Known \(4\)-chromatic unit-distance graphs (Moser spindle, Golomb graph, de Grey’s \(5\)-chromatic example) all contain triangles or \(4\)-cycles. “Blowing up” or sparsifying them while preserving unit distances and raising girth destroys the chromatic-number obstruction or introduces new short cycles.

No construction yielding, for every \(k\), a unit-distance graph of girth \(\geq k\) and chromatic number \(\geq 4\) has been found, nor has a proof that sufficiently large girth forces \(\chi\leq 3\) (via, e.g., discharging, algebraic independence of coordinates, or topological coloring arguments on the plane minus forbidden angles).

After examining the local angular constraints, the algebraic nature of cycle-closing conditions, greedy coloring on tree-like neighborhoods, and concrete attempts to build high-girth high-chromatic examples, the problem remains unresolved. I could not settle whether such a \(k\) exists.
