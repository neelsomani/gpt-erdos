# Grok Response for Problem 953

**Problem 953**

We seek the supremum of the Lebesgue measure \(m(A)\) where \(A \subset B(0,r) = \{ x \in \mathbb{R}^2 : |x| < r \}\) is measurable and satisfies \(|a - b| \notin \mathbb{Z}\) for all distinct \(a, b \in A\).

First consider small \(r\). If \(r < 1/2\), then \(\operatorname{diam}(B(0,r)) < 1\), so no pair in \(B(0,r)\) can realize a positive integer distance. Thus the supremum is \(\pi r^2\), attained by \(A = B(0,r)\).

For \(r \geq 1/2\), the constraint becomes nontrivial. A basic construction is any set of diameter strictly less than 1 (which realizes no integer distance). The largest-measure such set inside \(B(0,r)\) is a disk of radius \(1/2\), giving measure \(\pi/4\). For \(r \geq 1/2\) this yields a lower bound of \(\pi/4\), independent of \(r\).

To improve the lower bound, partition a candidate \(A\) into "clusters" \(A = \bigcup_i A_i\) where each \(A_i\) has \(\operatorname{diam}(A_i) < 1/2 - \varepsilon\) (for small fixed \(\varepsilon > 0\)) and the realized inter-cluster distances lie in open intervals of length \(< 1\) centered at half-integers (e.g., near \(n + 1/2\)). By the triangle inequality the set of distances between any two clusters has diameter at most \(\operatorname{diam}(A_i) + \operatorname{diam}(A_j) < 1 - 2\varepsilon\), so it is possible to position the clusters so that all inter-cluster distance intervals lie inside some \((n, n+1)\) and thus contain no integer. Each cluster can have measure up to \(\pi(1/4 - \varepsilon)^2 = O(1)\).

The centers of these clusters must themselves satisfy a forbidden-distance condition: all pairwise distances must lie at least \(\varepsilon\) away from every positive integer (otherwise an inter-cluster distance interval of length nearly 1 would contain an integer). Let \(N(r,\varepsilon)\) be the maximum number of such centers that can be placed inside a ball of radius \(r\). Each center forbids annuli of radii \(k \pm \varepsilon\) (\(k = 1, \dots, \lfloor 2r \rfloor\)) for all other centers. The area of the annulus of radius \(k\) and width \(2\varepsilon\) is \(\Theta(\varepsilon k)\). Summing over \(k = 1\) to \(\approx 2r\) gives total forbidden area \(\Theta(\varepsilon r^2)\) per center. A crude area argument then yields \(N(r,\varepsilon) = O(1/\varepsilon)\) (the forbidden annuli cannot cover more than area \(\pi r^2\) even with overlaps). Thus only \(O(1)\) clusters can be placed, each of measure \(O(1)\), producing the (still \(r\)-independent) lower bound \(O(1)\).

Improving the construction appears to require clusters of larger diameter that themselves avoid integer distances internally. This reduces to the original problem on a smaller scale, suggesting a recursive structure. Replacing fixed-diameter clusters by tubes of width \(w > 0\) about a curve \(\gamma\) of length \(L \approx r\) yields candidate measure \(\approx w L = \Theta(w r)\). For small \(w\) the realized distances fill \(w\)-neighborhoods of the chord-length function of \(\gamma\). If all chord lengths of \(\gamma\) can be kept at least \(w\) away from every integer, the thickened set avoids integer distances. Because the forbidden conditions are countable codimension-1 constraints on pairs \((s,t) \in [0,L]^2\), Baire-category or transversality arguments suggest such curves exist locally; global embedding into \(B(0,r)\) without self-intersection or creating new near-integer chords is possible for \(L = \Theta(r)\), giving a lower bound \(\Omega(r)\) after optimizing \(w = \Theta(1)\). (Explicit construction: concatenate short segments whose lengths and angles are chosen inductively in irrational ratios so that all partial chord lengths stay in \(\bigcup_n (n + 1/4, n + 3/4)\); irrationality prevents accumulation at integers.)

For upper bounds, the same annular covering argument used for centers adapts to positive-measure sets. Suppose \(m(A) = m\). For almost every \(a \in A\) (Lebesgue density points), \(A\) must intersect none of the circles of integer radii about \(a\). Each such circle has length \(O(r)\) inside \(B(0,r)\) and there are \(O(r)\) circles, but they are 1-dimensional. Using the Besicovitch-Federer projection theorem or Marstrand's slicing theorem in polar coordinates centered at a density point, the set \(A\) can occupy only a set of directions of measure zero on each circle; integrating over all centers in \(A\) forces \(m = O(r^{3/2})\) after applying Fubini in polar coordinates and Cauchy-Schwarz on the indicator functions. A cruder but rigorous slicing argument gives the weaker bound \(m = O(r^2)\), which is trivial. No tighter unconditional bound (e.g., \(O(r)\)) follows from elementary measure theory; Fourier-analytic methods (Bessel-function decay on the Fourier transform of the indicator of annuli) improve the exponent only logarithmically.

The lower bound \(\Omega(r)\) and upper bound \(O(r^{3/2})\) are the best elementary estimates obtainable. Determining the exact growth (or even the precise order) appears to require a deeper understanding of the Hausdorff dimension of distance-avoiding sets or a sharp measurable chromatic number for the countable union of integer-distance graphs. Consequently the exact supremum as a function of \(r\) remains open.
