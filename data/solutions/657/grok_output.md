The condition that \(A\) determines three distinct distances on every triple is equivalent to requiring that all distances from any fixed point of \(A\) to the remaining points are pairwise distinct. Combinatorially, this means the edges of \(K_n\) can be partitioned into \(D\) matchings (one per distance value), immediately yielding the lower bound \(D=\Omega(n)\) by double-counting the \(\binom{n}{2}\) edges against the maximum matching size \(\lfloor n/2\rfloor\). Geometrically realizing such a decomposition with only \(O(n)\) distinct Euclidean distances while keeping all inter-matching distance values consistent appears possible in principle (e.g., by placing large matchings of equal-length segments in irregular positions and orientations), but every concrete attempt (perturbations of regular polygons, dimers with controlled inter-cluster distances, generic points on a circle, exponentially spaced points on a line) either violates the no-isosceles condition or forces \(D=\omega(n)\). Standard incidence bounds (circles centered at points of \(A\) each containing exactly one other point of \(A\)) recover only the same \(\Omega(n)\) threshold and do not upgrade it to \(\omega(n)\). Thus the question of whether a function \(f(n)\to\infty\) exists remains open.
