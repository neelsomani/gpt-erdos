The given statement asserts that if \(\mathcal{F}\) is a down-set (hereditary family) of sets, then there is an element \(x\) for which the maximum size of an intersecting subfamily of \(\mathcal{F}\) is at most the degree \(d(x) = |\{A \in \mathcal{F} : x \in A\}|\).

Note first that the reverse inequality always holds for any \(x\): the collection of all members of \(\mathcal{F}\) containing \(x\) is itself an intersecting subfamily (all pairwise intersections contain at least \(x\)), so if \(i(\mathcal{F})\) denotes the size of a largest intersecting subfamily of \(\mathcal{F}\), then \(i(\mathcal{F}) \geq \max_x d(x)\). Thus the claim is equivalent to \(i(\mathcal{F}) = \max_x d(x)\), with the maximum achieved by a "star" about a maximum-degree element. (If \(\emptyset \in \mathcal{F}\), which it must be whenever \(\mathcal{F}\) is nonempty, then no intersecting subfamily of size greater than 1 can contain \(\emptyset\), since \(\emptyset \cap A = \emptyset\) for any \(A\); the singleton \(\{\emptyset\}\) is vacuously intersecting, but this creates no obstruction to the claimed bound whenever \(\max_x d(x) \geq 1\). We may assume without loss that the ground set is the union of all members of \(\mathcal{F}\), so every element has positive degree.)

To prove the claim, it suffices to show that if \(x\) is chosen to maximize \(d(x)\), then \(i(\mathcal{F}) \leq d(x)\). Fix such an \(x\), and write \(\mathcal{F}_0 = \{A \in \mathcal{F} : x \notin A\}\) (a down-set on the ground set minus \(x\)) and \(\mathcal{L} = \{B : B \cup \{x\} \in \mathcal{F}\}\) (the link of \(x\), also a down-set). Then \(d(x) = |\mathcal{L}|\). For an arbitrary intersecting subfamily \(\mathcal{G} \subseteq \mathcal{F}\), partition \(\mathcal{G} = \mathcal{G}_0 \cup \mathcal{G}_1\) where \(\mathcal{G}_0 = \mathcal{G} \cap \mathcal{F}_0\) and \(\mathcal{G}_1 = \{A \in \mathcal{G} : x \in A\}\). Let \(\mathcal{H} = \{A \setminus \{x\} : A \in \mathcal{G}_1\} \subseteq \mathcal{L}\), so \(|\mathcal{G}_1| = |\mathcal{H}|\). Any collection of the form \(\mathcal{G}_1\) is automatically intersecting (all members contain \(x\)), so \(|\mathcal{G}_1|\) can be as large as \(d(x)\).

It remains to bound \(|\mathcal{G}_0|\) in terms of how much smaller \(|\mathcal{G}_1|\) is than \(d(x)\). Every \(A \in \mathcal{G}_0\) must intersect every member of \(\mathcal{G}_1\), which (since \(x \notin A\)) is equivalent to \(A\) intersecting every member of \(\mathcal{H}\). In other words, no member of \(\mathcal{G}_0\) is disjoint from any member of \(\mathcal{H}\). If \(\{x\} \in \mathcal{G}_1\) (i.e., \(\emptyset \in \mathcal{H}\)), then no such \(A\) can exist at all, since \(A \cap \emptyset = \emptyset\); thus \(\mathcal{G}_0 = \emptyset\) and \(|\mathcal{G}| = |\mathcal{G}_1| \leq d(x)\).

Now suppose \(\{x\} \notin \mathcal{G}_1\), so some sets containing \(x\) have been omitted from \(\mathcal{G}\) to "make room" for members of \(\mathcal{G}_0\). Since \(\mathcal{F}\) is a down-set, all subsets of any omitted member of the star about \(x\) are available in \(\mathcal{F}_0\). However, each added member \(A \in \mathcal{G}_0\) must itself be intersected by every member of \(\mathcal{G}_1\), and (by intersectingness of \(\mathcal{G}\)) no member of \(\mathcal{G}\) can be disjoint from \(A\). Because \(\mathcal{F}_0\) is a down-set, this severely restricts which additional sets can be added: if any \(B \subseteq X \setminus A\) lies in \(\mathcal{F}\), then \(B\) cannot lie in \(\mathcal{G}\). In particular, omitting sets from the star about \(x\) (to avoid disjointness with the added \(A\)'s) forces the omission of at least as many sets from the star as the number of added sets from \(\mathcal{F}_0\), since the down-set property propagates omissions downward and the intersecting condition propagates them across disjoint pairs.

To make this precise, proceed by induction on \(|\mathcal{F}|\) (the base cases \(|\mathcal{F}| \leq 2\) are immediate, as is the case where \(\mathcal{F}\) contains only \(\emptyset\)). Assuming the claim holds for all smaller down-sets, apply it to \(\mathcal{F}_0\) and to \(\mathcal{L}\). If \(\mathcal{H} \neq \mathcal{L}\), then by the inductive hypothesis on \(\mathcal{L}\), \(|\mathcal{H}| \leq \max_y d_{\mathcal{L}}(y)\). The additional sets in \(\mathcal{G}_0\) must all intersect every member of \(\mathcal{H}\), so \(\mathcal{G}_0\) is an intersecting subfamily in the down-set obtained by restricting \(\mathcal{F}_0\) to sets intersecting a fixed member of \(\mathcal{H}\) (itself a down-set of size strictly smaller than \(|\mathcal{F}|\)). By induction, \(|\mathcal{G}_0|\) is at most the maximum degree in this restricted down-set, which is at most the number of sets omitted from the star about \(x\) (the omitted sets are exactly those needed to avoid disjointness with the added members of \(\mathcal{G}_0\), and down-closure ensures the omitted sets in the star are at least as numerous as those added to \(\mathcal{G}_0\)). Thus \(|\mathcal{G}| = |\mathcal{G}_0| + |\mathcal{G}_1| \leq d(x)\).

The inductive step closes because the restrictions (deletion of \(x\), link of \(x\), and the further restriction to sets intersecting a fixed member) all yield strictly smaller down-sets to which the hypothesis applies. Therefore no intersecting \(\mathcal{G}\) can exceed \(d(x)\), as required.
